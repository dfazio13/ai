SECTION 1. WHAT IS CORPORATE FINANCE?
================================================================================

1.1 FUNDAMENTAL DEFINITION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Corporate finance is the study of how firms allocate scarce resources over time
under conditions of uncertainty to maximize value.

Damodaran's Definition:
"Every decision that affects the finances of a business is a corporate finance
decision."

Key Insight:
Corporate finance is NOT just about raising money or managing investments. It's
about ALL decisions that have financial implications, including:
- Operations (pricing, capacity, inventory)
- Marketing (product launches, advertising)
- Human resources (hiring, compensation)
- Strategy (M&A, divestitures, partnerships)

1.2 THE THREE PILLARS OF CORPORATE FINANCE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PILLAR 1: INVESTMENT DECISIONS (Capital Budgeting)
Question: Which projects should the firm undertake?
Focus: Asset side of the balance sheet
Key Tool: Net Present Value (NPV)

Examples:
- Should we build a new factory?
- Should we launch this new product?
- Should we acquire this company?
- Should we upgrade our technology?

PILLAR 2: FINANCING DECISIONS (Capital Structure)
Question: How should the firm fund these projects?
Focus: Liability and equity side of the balance sheet
Key Tool: Cost of Capital (WACC)

Examples:
- Should we issue debt or equity?
- What is the optimal debt-to-equity ratio?
- Should we use bank loans or corporate bonds?
- When should we refinance existing debt?

PILLAR 3: PAYOUT DECISIONS (Dividend Policy)
Question: How should cash be returned to investors?
Focus: Distribution of free cash flows
Key Tool: Residual dividend model

Examples:
- Should we pay dividends or repurchase shares?
- How much cash should we retain for growth?
- When should we initiate/increase dividends?
- Should we use special dividends or regular dividends?

1.3 REAL-WORLD EXAMPLES OF CORPORATE FINANCE DECISIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Example 1: Apple's Product Development
Decision: Invest $1 billion in developing the iPhone
Type: Investment decision
Analysis: Estimate development costs, projected sales, profit margins, and
calculate NPV using appropriate discount rate

Example 2: Netflix's Debt Financing
Decision: Issue $2 billion in bonds to fund content production
Type: Financing decision
Analysis: Compare cost of debt vs. equity, evaluate impact on leverage,
assess effect on WACC

Example 3: Microsoft's Dividend Increase
Decision: Increase quarterly dividend from $0.56 to $0.62 per share
Type: Payout decision
Analysis: Evaluate available cash, future investment needs, shareholder
preferences, and tax implications

1.4 COMMON STUDENT MISCONCEPTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

MISCONCEPTION 1: "Corporate finance is only for CFOs and finance departments"
REALITY: Every manager makes decisions with financial implications. Marketing,
operations, and strategy all require understanding of value creation.

MISCONCEPTION 2: "More revenue always means better decisions"
REALITY: Revenue growth that costs more than it's worth destroys value. A
project generating $10M in revenue with $12M in costs is a bad investment.

MISCONCEPTION 3: "Profitable companies don't need corporate finance"
REALITY: Even profitable firms must allocate capital efficiently. Choosing
the BEST projects, not just profitable ones, determines success.

MISCONCEPTION 4: "Corporate finance is purely quantitative"
REALITY: While math is essential, judgment, strategic thinking, and
qualitative assessment are equally important.

1.5 TEACHING NOTES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
When introducing corporate finance:
1. Start with intuitive examples students can relate to (e.g., college education
   as an investment decision)
2. Emphasize that CF is about decision-making, not accounting
3. Use business headlines to show relevance
4. Preview that all three pillars are interconnected
5. Explain that this course focuses primarily on Pillar 1 (investment decisions)

SECTION 2. THE OBJECTIVE OF THE FIRM
================================================================================

2.1 VALUE MAXIMIZATION PRINCIPLE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Standard Assumption in Corporate Finance:
The objective of the firm is to MAXIMIZE FIRM VALUE.

More precisely:
Maximize the present value of expected future cash flows to all stakeholders
(equity holders and debt holders).

Mathematical Expression:
Firm Value = PV(All Future Cash Flows)

This objective:
✓ Is descriptive of how capital markets reward managers
✓ Provides a clear and consistent decision rule
✓ Aligns managerial incentives with shareholder wealth
✓ Is measurable and quantifiable
✓ Facilitates comparison across alternatives

2.2 WHY VALUE MAXIMIZATION?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

REASON 1: Market Discipline
- Stock prices reflect value creation/destruction
- Managers who don't maximize value face:
  * Hostile takeovers
  * Board pressure
  * Job loss
  * Compensation penalties

REASON 2: Clarity
- Provides unambiguous guidance for decision-making
- NPV > 0 → Accept
- NPV < 0 → Reject

REASON 3: Shareholder Primacy
- Shareholders are residual claimants
- Maximizing equity value (given debt obligations) maximizes firm value
- Other stakeholders are protected by contracts

REASON 4: Efficiency
- Leads to optimal resource allocation in the economy
- Capital flows to highest-value uses

2.3 VALUE CREATION PRINCIPLE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A firm creates value when:
PV(Benefits) > PV(Costs)

Or equivalently:
NPV = PV(Benefits) - PV(Costs) > 0

Key Insight:
"Good" decisions are NOT those that:
- Increase revenue (revenue may cost too much)
- Increase profit (profit may not justify investment)
- Increase market share (share may be bought at negative NPV)

"Good" decisions are those that increase firm value (NPV > 0).

2.4 BAD DECISIONS IN PRACTICE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Example 1: Revenue Growth Without Value Creation
A company spends $100M on a marketing campaign that generates $80M in
additional revenue with 40% margins.

Revenue increase: $80M ✓
Profit increase: $32M ✓
Cost: $100M
Net effect: -$68M ✗
Decision: BAD (destroys value)

Example 2: Accounting Profit vs. Economic Profit
A company invests $1M in a project that generates $120K in annual
accounting profit indefinitely.

Accounting ROI: 12% per year ✓
Opportunity cost of capital: 15%
NPV at 15%: $1M - $120K/0.15 = $1M - $800K = -$200K ✗
Decision: BAD (negative NPV despite positive accounting profit)

Example 3: Market Share at Any Cost
A company offers aggressive discounts to gain market share, reducing margins
below the cost of capital.

Market share: Up ✓
Revenue: Up ✓
Profit margins: Down ✗
ROIC: Below WACC ✗
Decision: BAD (destroys value despite market share gains)

2.5 ALTERNATIVE OBJECTIVES (DISCUSSION)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Some argue firms should maximize OTHER objectives:

ALTERNATIVE 1: Profit Maximization
Problem: Ignores timing and risk of cash flows
Critique: $1M profit in 1 year ≠ $1M profit in 10 years

ALTERNATIVE 2: Market Share Maximization
Problem: May require negative NPV investments
Critique: Dominant market position with negative returns destroys value

ALTERNATIVE 3: Stakeholder Value (ESG)
Problem: Multiple conflicting objectives
Critique: Without clear trade-offs, leads to inconsistent decisions
Note: ESG can be incorporated into value maximization framework

ALTERNATIVE 4: Sales/Revenue Growth
Problem: Ignores profitability and capital requirements
Critique: Growth that costs more than it's worth destroys value

2.6 COMMON STUDENT MISCONCEPTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

MISCONCEPTION 1: "Maximizing profit is the same as maximizing value"
REALITY: Profit ignores timing, risk, and required investment. A project with
$1M profit requiring $20M investment at 10% cost of capital has negative NPV.

MISCONCEPTION 2: "Value maximization means short-term focus"
REALITY: NPV considers ALL future cash flows. Long-term projects can have
positive NPV. The issue is whether long-term investments create value.

MISCONCEPTION 3: "Companies should maximize shareholder value at all costs"
REALITY: Legal and ethical constraints matter. The objective is to maximize
value WITHIN the bounds of law and social responsibility.

MISCONCEPTION 4: "Non-financial goals are incompatible with value maximization"
REALITY: Customer satisfaction, employee welfare, and environmental
responsibility can enhance long-term value. The question is whether the
benefits justify the costs.

2.7 TEACHING NOTES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
When teaching the objective of the firm:
1. Acknowledge that real-world objectives are more complex
2. Explain that value maximization is a working assumption, not dogma
3. Use examples where revenue growth or profit growth destroys value
4. Discuss agency problems (managers vs. shareholders)
5. Connect to students' personal decisions (college as value-maximizing investment)
6. Introduce the concept that value maximization is "first-order" important

SECTION 3. CASH FLOWS AND VALUE CREATION
================================================================================

3.1 FUNDAMENTAL PRINCIPLE: CASH IS KING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Core Principle:
Value depends on CASH FLOWS, not accounting profits.

Why This Matters:
Investors consume cash, not earnings. You cannot spend accounting profit.

Cash Flow vs. Accounting Profit:
Cash Flow = Money received or paid
Accounting Profit = Revenue minus expenses (including non-cash items)

The Disconnect:
A company can be profitable on paper but run out of cash (and go bankrupt).
A company can have negative accounting profit but generate positive cash flow.

3.2 WHY ACCOUNTING PROFITS ARE INSUFFICIENT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

REASON 1: Non-Cash Expenses
Accounting profit includes depreciation and amortization, which don't represent
actual cash outflows in the current period.

Example:
Revenue: $1,000
Cash expenses: $600
Depreciation: $200
Accounting profit: $200
Cash flow: $400 (ignoring taxes)

REASON 2: Timing Differences
Accounting uses accrual basis. Revenue is recognized when earned, not when
cash is received. Expenses are matched to revenues, not to cash payments.

Example:
Year 1: Sell product for $1,000 on credit
Accounting: Record $1,000 revenue
Cash flow: $0 (if customer hasn't paid)

Year 2: Customer pays $1,000
Accounting: No additional revenue
Cash flow: $1,000

REASON 3: Capital Expenditures
Investment in PP&E is NOT expensed immediately in accounting but IS a cash
outflow that reduces cash available to investors.

Example:
Company buys $1M machine with 10-year life
Accounting expense (annual): $100K depreciation
Cash outflow: $1M in year 0

REASON 4: Working Capital Changes
Increases in inventory and accounts receivable tie up cash even though they
may not impact accounting profit immediately.

REASON 5: Accounting Choices
Different depreciation methods, inventory valuation (FIFO vs. LIFO), revenue
recognition policies create artificial differences across firms.

3.3 RELEVANT CASH FLOWS FOR VALUATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Cash flows must be:

CHARACTERISTIC 1: INCREMENTAL
Only include cash flows that occur because of the decision.

Ask: "What changes if we do the project vs. not doing it?"

Include:
✓ Additional revenues from the project
✓ Additional costs from the project
✓ Opportunity costs (forgone alternatives)

Exclude:
✗ Sunk costs (already incurred, cannot be recovered)
✗ Allocated overhead (unless it actually increases)
✗ Financing costs (handled through discount rate)

CHARACTERISTIC 2: AFTER-TAX
Cash flows should reflect actual tax payments/savings.

Formula:
After-tax cash flow = Pre-tax cash flow × (1 - tax rate)

Why: Investors receive after-tax cash flows.

CHARACTERISTIC 3: EXPECTED (NOT REALIZED)
Use expected values, not best-case or worst-case scenarios.

If uncertain:
Expected CF = Σ [Probability × Cash Flow in each scenario]

CHARACTERISTIC 4: INCLUDE OPPORTUNITY COSTS
The value of the next-best alternative must be considered.

Example:
Using land the company already owns? Include the market value of the land
(what you could sell it for) as an opportunity cost.

3.4 COMMON CASH FLOW ERRORS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ERROR 1: Including Sunk Costs
Sunk costs are irrelevant for decision-making.

Example:
Company spent $500K on R&D for a product. Should they launch?
WRONG: Include $500K in project costs
RIGHT: Ignore $500K (it's spent whether we launch or not)

ERROR 2: Forgetting Opportunity Costs
Opportunity costs are real economic costs even if no cash changes hands.

Example:
Using warehouse we own for new project.
WRONG: Assign zero cost because we own it
RIGHT: Include market rental value (what we could earn leasing it out)

ERROR 3: Including Interest Expense
Interest is a financing decision, not an operating decision.

Why wrong: Double-counting. Interest is already reflected in the discount rate.

ERROR 4: Ignoring Working Capital
Changes in working capital affect cash flow.

Increase in WC = Cash outflow (cash tied up)
Decrease in WC = Cash inflow (cash freed up)

ERROR 5: Using Accounting Numbers Directly
Must adjust from accounting profit to cash flow.

Adjustments needed:
+ Depreciation & Amortization (non-cash)
- Capital Expenditures (cash outflow not expensed)
- Increase in Net Working Capital (cash tied up)

3.5 FROM ACCOUNTING PROFIT TO CASH FLOW
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Standard Formula:
Free Cash Flow = EBIT × (1 - Tax Rate)
                 + Depreciation
                 - Capital Expenditures
                 - Increase in Net Working Capital

Step-by-Step Logic:
1. Start with EBIT (operating profit)
2. Tax it: EBIT × (1 - τ)
3. Add back non-cash expenses: + Depreciation
4. Subtract cash investments: - CapEx
5. Subtract increases in working capital: - ΔNWC

Alternative Formula (from Net Income):
Free Cash Flow = Net Income
                 + Depreciation
                 + Interest × (1 - τ)
                 - Capital Expenditures
                 - Increase in Net Working Capital

Note: We add back after-tax interest because FCF is cash available to ALL
investors (debt and equity), before interest payments.

3.6 WORKING CAPITAL DETAILS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Net Working Capital (NWC) = Current Assets - Current Liabilities

More specifically:
NWC = (Accounts Receivable + Inventory) - Accounts Payable

Change in NWC:
ΔNWC = NWC(end) - NWC(beginning)

Impact on Cash Flow:
- If NWC increases: Cash flow DECREASES (cash is tied up)
- If NWC decreases: Cash flow INCREASES (cash is freed up)

Example:
Year 0: NWC = $100K
Year 1: NWC = $150K
ΔNWC = $50K
Impact: -$50K cash flow (we need to invest $50K more in working capital)

At Project End:
Assume all working capital is recovered (NWC goes to zero).
This is a CASH INFLOW in the terminal year.

3.7 PRACTICAL EXAMPLE: COMPLETE CASH FLOW CALCULATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Scenario:
Company considering new product line.
- Initial equipment cost: $1,000K (depreciated straight-line over 5 years)
- Expected annual revenue: $800K
- Expected annual costs (excluding depreciation): $300K
- Tax rate: 30%
- Initial working capital: $100K
- Project life: 5 years
- Salvage value of equipment: $100K
- Working capital recovered at end: $100K

Solution:

Year 0 Cash Flow:
Initial investment: -$1,000K
Initial working capital: -$100K
Total: -$1,100K

Years 1-4 Cash Flow (annual):
Revenue: $800K
Costs: -$300K
Depreciation: -$200K ($1,000K / 5 years)
EBIT: $300K
Tax (30%): -$90K
NOPAT: $210K
+ Depreciation: +$200K (add back non-cash expense)
Free Cash Flow: $410K per year

Year 5 Cash Flow:
Operating cash flow: $410K (same as years 1-4)
Salvage value: $100K (after-tax if applicable)
Working capital recovery: $100K
Total Year 5 Cash Flow: $610K

Summary:
Year 0: -$1,100K
Years 1-4: +$410K each
Year 5: +$610K

3.8 COMMON STUDENT MISCONCEPTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

MISCONCEPTION 1: "Depreciation reduces cash flow"
REALITY: Depreciation is a non-cash expense. It INCREASES cash flow because
it reduces taxes (creates a tax shield).

MISCONCEPTION 2: "Sunk costs should be included to 'recover' them"
REALITY: Sunk costs are irrelevant. Including them leads to bad decisions.

MISCONCEPTION 3: "We own the asset, so it's free to use"
REALITY: Using owned assets has an opportunity cost (what you could earn by
selling or renting it).

MISCONCEPTION 4: "Profit and cash flow are basically the same"
REALITY: They can diverge significantly due to non-cash items, timing, and
capital investments.

MISCONCEPTION 5: "Working capital doesn't matter much"
REALITY: Working capital can be substantial, especially for growing firms or
retail businesses.

3.9 TEACHING NOTES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
When teaching cash flows:
1. Start with simple examples showing accounting profit ≠ cash flow
2. Use the "Can you spend it?" test
3. Work through complete examples step-by-step
4. Emphasize that EVERY cash flow must be incremental
5. Practice identifying sunk costs vs. opportunity costs
6. Show how depreciation tax shields increase value
7. Explain working capital using intuitive examples (retail store expansion)

Critical Teaching Moment:
Many students mechanically apply formulas without understanding. Require them
to explain in words WHY each adjustment is made.

SECTION 4. TIME VALUE OF MONEY
================================================================================

4.1 FUNDAMENTAL CONCEPT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Core Principle:
A dollar today is worth MORE than a dollar tomorrow.

This is perhaps THE most fundamental concept in finance.

Implications:
- We cannot simply add cash flows occurring at different times
- Future cash flows must be "discounted" to present values
- The discount rate reflects the time value of money

Formula:
PV = FV / (1 + r)^t

Where:
PV = Present Value
FV = Future Value
r = discount rate (per period)
t = number of periods

4.2 WHY MONEY HAS TIME VALUE: THE THREE REASONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

REASON 1: OPPORTUNITY COST (Positive Interest Rates)
Money received today can be invested to earn a return.

Example:
Receive $100 today → Invest at 10% → Have $110 in one year
Receive $100 in one year → Have $100 in one year

Therefore: $100 today > $100 in one year

Implication:
To be indifferent, the future payment must compensate for the forgone
investment return.

REASON 2: RISK AND UNCERTAINTY
Future cash flows are uncertain. There's risk you might not receive them.

Sources of Uncertainty:
- Business risk (project might fail)
- Default risk (borrower might not pay)
- Economic risk (recession might occur)

Implication:
Risky future cash flows are worth less than certain current cash flows.
Higher risk → larger discount → lower present value.

REASON 3: INFLATION
The purchasing power of money declines over time.

Example:
$100 today buys a basket of goods
$100 in one year (with 3% inflation) buys 3% less

Implication:
To maintain purchasing power, future cash must be larger to compensate.

Note: In most corporate finance applications, we use NOMINAL cash flows and
NOMINAL discount rates (both including inflation).

4.3 PRESENT VALUE: THE FUNDAMENTAL TOOL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Definition:
Present Value is the value TODAY of a stream of future cash flows.

Single Cash Flow:
PV = CF / (1 + r)^t

Multiple Cash Flows:
PV = CF₁/(1+r)¹ + CF₂/(1+r)² + ... + CFₙ/(1+r)ⁿ

Or:
PV = Σ [CFₜ / (1+r)^t]  for t = 1 to n

Interpretation:
PV answers: "How much would I pay today for the right to receive these future
cash flows?"

4.4 FUTURE VALUE: THE INVERSE OPERATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Definition:
Future Value is the value AT A FUTURE DATE of cash flows received earlier.

Single Cash Flow:
FV = PV × (1 + r)^t

Multiple Cash Flows (future value at time T):
FV = CF₁(1+r)^(T-1) + CF₂(1+r)^(T-2) + ... + CFₙ(1+r)^(T-n)

Interpretation:
FV answers: "If I invest today, how much will I have in the future?"

Relationship:
PV and FV are inverses:
FV = PV × (1 + r)^t
PV = FV / (1 + r)^t

4.5 THE DISCOUNT RATE: WHAT DETERMINES IT?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The discount rate (r) reflects TWO things:
1. Time preference (opportunity cost of waiting)
2. Risk (uncertainty of receiving the cash flow)

Components of Discount Rate:
r = Risk-free rate + Risk premium

Risk-free rate:
- Compensation for time (delay)
- Typically approximated by government bond yields

Risk premium:
- Compensation for uncertainty
- Higher for riskier projects
- Reflects systematic (non-diversifiable) risk

Key Principle:
Different cash flows require different discount rates based on their risk.
Safe cash flows → low r
Risky cash flows → high r

4.6 COMPOUNDING FREQUENCY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Most finance problems assume ANNUAL compounding, but other frequencies exist.

Annual Compounding:
FV = PV × (1 + r)^t

Semi-annual Compounding:
FV = PV × (1 + r/2)^(2t)

Quarterly Compounding:
FV = PV × (1 + r/4)^(4t)

Monthly Compounding:
FV = PV × (1 + r/12)^(12t)

Continuous Compounding:
FV = PV × e^(rt)

Effective Annual Rate (EAR):
The actual annual rate after accounting for compounding within the year.

EAR = (1 + r/m)^m - 1

Where m = number of compounding periods per year.

Example:
Nominal rate: 12% per year, compounded monthly
EAR = (1 + 0.12/12)^12 - 1 = 1.01^12 - 1 ≈ 12.68%

4.7 PRACTICAL EXAMPLES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Example 1: Simple Present Value
You will receive $10,000 in 5 years. Discount rate is 8%.
What is this worth today?

Solution:
PV = $10,000 / (1.08)^5
PV = $10,000 / 1.469
PV = $6,806

Interpretation: $6,806 today is equivalent to $10,000 in 5 years at 8%.

Example 2: Multiple Cash Flows
You will receive $1,000 in year 1, $2,000 in year 2, and $3,000 in year 3.
Discount rate is 10%. What is the present value?

Solution:
PV = $1,000/(1.10)¹ + $2,000/(1.10)² + $3,000/(1.10)³
PV = $909 + $1,653 + $2,254
PV = $4,816

Example 3: Impact of Risk on Discount Rate
Same cash flows as Example 2, but now discount rate is 15% (riskier).

Solution:
PV = $1,000/(1.15)¹ + $2,000/(1.15)² + $3,000/(1.15)³
PV = $870 + $1,512 + $1,973
PV = $4,355

Notice: Higher risk (higher discount rate) → Lower present value
Difference: $4,816 - $4,355 = $461 (almost 10% less value)

4.8 COMMON STUDENT MISCONCEPTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

MISCONCEPTION 1: "Future value is always higher than present value"
REALITY: With negative discount rates (rare), future value can be lower.
Also, this is only true for positive cash flows.

MISCONCEPTION 2: "Time value of money only matters for long periods"
REALITY: Even short time periods matter, especially with high discount rates
or large amounts.

MISCONCEPTION 3: "All cash flows should use the same discount rate"
REALITY: Riskier cash flows require higher discount rates. The discount rate
must match the risk of the cash flows.

MISCONCEPTION 4: "Compounding frequency doesn't matter much"
REALITY: With high rates or long periods, compounding frequency significantly
affects outcomes.

MISCONCEPTION 5: "Present value means today's market price"
REALITY: PV is what something SHOULD be worth given expected cash flows and
appropriate discount rate. Market price may differ due to mispricing.

4.9 TEACHING NOTES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
When teaching time value of money:
1. Start with intuitive examples (Would you rather have $100 today or $100
   next year? Why?)
2. Show how small differences in discount rates or time periods compound
3. Use timelines to visualize cash flows
4. Emphasize that discounting and compounding are inverse operations
5. Practice with varied examples (positive and negative cash flows, different
   timing patterns)
6. Demonstrate sensitivity to discount rate changes
7. Connect to students' lives (student loans, savings, retirement planning)

Critical Concept:
The discount rate is NOT arbitrary. It reflects opportunity cost and risk.
Using the wrong discount rate leads to bad decisions.

SECTION 5. PRESENT VALUE FORMULAS AND SHORTCUTS
================================================================================

5.1 GENERAL PRESENT VALUE FORMULA
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

For any series of cash flows:
PV = Σ [CFₜ / (1+r)^t]  for t = 1 to n

This is the fundamental formula. All other formulas are special cases.

When to use:
- When cash flows are irregular
- When no pattern exists
- When other shortcuts don't apply

Limitations:
- Requires calculation for each period
- Can be tedious for long periods

5.2 PERPETUITIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Definition:
A perpetuity is a constant cash flow that continues forever.

Formula:
PV = C / r

Where:
C = constant cash flow per period
r = discount rate per period

Derivation (Intuition):
If you invest PV = C/r at rate r, you earn C per period forever without
depleting principal.

Example:
Annual cash flow: $1,000
Discount rate: 10%
PV = $1,000 / 0.10 = $10,000

Check: $10,000 invested at 10% earns $1,000 per year forever ✓

When to Use Perpetuities:
1. Valuing very long-lived cash flows (>50 years)
2. Terminal value calculations in DCF models
3. Valuing certain types of bonds (consols)
4. Approximating firm value with stable cash flows

Important Note:
"Perpetuity" does NOT mean infinite life literally. It's an approximation.
For practical purposes, 50 years ≈ forever because distant cash flows have
minimal present value.

Mathematical Insight:
Why does the formula work?

PV = C/(1+r) + C/(1+r)² + C/(1+r)³ + ...

This is a geometric series with first term a = C/(1+r) and ratio x = 1/(1+r).

Sum of infinite geometric series = a / (1-x)

PV = [C/(1+r)] / [1 - 1/(1+r)]
PV = C/(1+r) × [(1+r)/r]
PV = C/r ✓

5.3 GROWING PERPETUITIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Definition:
A cash flow that grows at constant rate g forever.

Formula:
PV = C / (r - g)

Where:
C = cash flow in first period
r = discount rate
g = growth rate

Critical Condition:
r > g (discount rate must exceed growth rate)

Why: If g ≥ r, the present value would be infinite (mathematically) or
undefined (economically).

Example:
First cash flow: $1,000
Growth rate: 3% per year
Discount rate: 10%

PV = $1,000 / (0.10 - 0.03) = $1,000 / 0.07 = $14,286

Check the cash flows:
Year 1: $1,000
Year 2: $1,030 (grows at 3%)
Year 3: $1,061
And so on...

Applications:
1. Terminal value in DCF (most important application)
2. Dividend discount model for stable firms
3. Real estate valuation with rent growth
4. Infrastructure valuations

Economic Interpretation:
The growing perpetuity captures both:
- Initial cash yield (C/V)
- Expected growth (g)
Therefore: r = C/V + g (similar to dividend yield plus growth)

Derivation (Algebraic):
PV = C/(1+r) + Cg)/(1+r)² + C(1+g)²/(1+r)³ + ...
PV = C/(1+r) × [1 + (1+g)/(1+r) + ((1+g)/(1+r))² + ...]

This is a geometric series with ratio = (1+g)/(1+r)

PV = [C/(1+r)] / [1 - (1+g)/(1+r)]
PV = C/(1+r) × [(1+r)/(r-g)]
PV = C / (r-g) ✓

5.4 ANNUITIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Definition:
An annuity is a constant cash flow for a FINITE number of periods.

Formula:
PV = (C/r) × [1 - 1/(1+r)^T]

Alternative form:
PV = C × [(1 - (1+r)^(-T)) / r]

Where:
C = constant cash flow per period
r = discount rate
T = number of periods

Intuition:
PV(annuity) = PV(perpetuity) - PV(perpetuity starting at T+1)

PV = C/r - [C/r × 1/(1+r)^T]
PV = (C/r) × [1 - 1/(1+r)^T] ✓

Example:
Annual payment: $5,000
Discount rate: 8%
Number of years: 10

PV = ($5,000/0.08) × [1 - 1/(1.08)^10]
PV = $62,500 × [1 - 0.463]
PV = $62,500 × 0.537
PV = $33,550

Applications:
1. Mortgage payments
2. Car loans
3. Lease payments
4. Bond coupon payments
5. Lottery winnings (if paid over time)
6. Retirement annuities

Annuity Due vs. Ordinary Annuity:
- Ordinary annuity: Payments at END of each period (most common assumption)
- Annuity due: Payments at BEGINNING of each period

PV(annuity due) = PV(ordinary annuity) × (1 + r)

Why: Each payment comes one period earlier, so multiply by (1+r).

5.5 GROWING ANNUITIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Definition:
A cash flow that grows at constant rate g for T periods.

Formula:
PV = C/(r-g) × [1 - ((1+g)/(1+r))^T]

Where:
C = first period cash flow
r = discount rate
g = growth rate
T = number of periods

Condition:
Works for any r and g (r does not need to exceed g for finite periods)

Example:
First payment: $1,000
Growth rate: 5%
Discount rate: 10%
Number of periods: 20

PV = $1,000/(0.10-0.05) × [1 - (1.05/1.10)^20]
PV = $1,000/0.05 × [1 - 0.377]
PV = $20,000 × 0.623
PV = $12,460

Applications:
- Valuing cash flows with expected growth (revenues, dividends)
- Salary projections with inflation
- Retirement planning with growing expenses

Special Case: When g = r
PV = C × T / (1+r)

Derivation is more complex; requires L'Hôpital's rule.

5.6 WHICH FORMULA TO USE? DECISION TREE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

START: Do you have a stream of cash flows?
│
├─ NO → Use single CF formula: PV = CF / (1+r)^t
│
└─ YES → Are cash flows constant (same amount each period)?
    │
    ├─ NO → Are cash flows growing at constant rate?
    │   │
    │   ├─ NO → Use general formula: PV = Σ [CFₜ / (1+r)^t]
    │   │
    │   └─ YES → Do they continue forever?
    │       │
    │       ├─ YES → Growing perpetuity: PV = C/(r-g)
    │       │
    │       └─ NO → Growing annuity: PV = C/(r-g) × [1 - ((1+g)/(1+r))^T]
    │
    └─ YES (Constant CFs) → Do they continue forever?
        │
        ├─ YES → Perpetuity: PV = C/r
        │
        └─ NO → Annuity: PV = (C/r) × [1 - 1/(1+r)^T]

5.7 PRACTICAL EXAMPLES WITH MULTIPLE FORMULAS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Example 1: Retirement Planning
You need $50,000 per year in retirement for 30 years.
How much do you need to save if you earn 6% per year?

Solution:
This is an annuity (constant payment for finite period).
PV = ($50,000/0.06) × [1 - 1/(1.06)^30]
PV = $833,333 × [1 - 0.174]
PV = $833,333 × 0.826
PV = $688,195

Example 2: Real Estate Valuation
A property generates $100,000 rent per year, growing at 2% per year forever.
Required return is 8%. What is it worth?

Solution:
This is a growing perpetuity.
PV = $100,000 / (0.08 - 0.02)
PV = $100,000 / 0.06
PV = $1,666,667

Example 3: Lottery Winnings
You win $10M, paid as $500K per year for 20 years.
What is the present value if discount rate is 5%?

Solution:
This is an annuity.
PV = ($500,000/0.05) × [1 - 1/(1.05)^20]
PV = $10,000,000 × [1 - 0.377]
PV = $10,000,000 × 0.623
PV = $6,230,000

Note: The "advertised" $10M is not the real value. Present value is $6.23M.

5.8 COMMON STUDENT ERRORS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ERROR 1: Using perpetuity formula when cash flows are finite
WRONG: PV = C/r for 20 years of cash flows
RIGHT: Use annuity formula

ERROR 2: Forgetting that C is the FIRST cash flow in growing perpetuity
WRONG: Discounting first cash flow again
RIGHT: C is already the year 1 cash flow

ERROR 3: Using growing perpetuity when g > r
WRONG: PV = $100 / (0.05 - 0.08) = -$3,333 ???
RIGHT: Formula doesn't apply. Either project is infinite value or g is not
sustainable.

ERROR 4: Mixing up annuity and perpetuity formulas
Common mistake: Using C/r when cash flows are finite

ERROR 5: Using nominal cash flows with real discount rates (or vice versa)
WRONG: Real cash flows with nominal discount rate
RIGHT: Match real with real, or nominal with nominal

5.9 TEACHING NOTES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
When teaching PV formulas:
1. Start with general formula, then show shortcuts
2. Emphasize that shortcuts are just conveniences
3. Use timelines to visualize patterns
4. Practice identifying which formula applies
5. Show how formulas are derived (helps with understanding)
6. Warn against mechanically applying formulas without thinking
7. Demonstrate sensitivity to parameters (r, g, T)

Memory Aid for Students:
"PGAP" - Perpetuity, Growing Perpetuity, Annuity, General
- Perpetuity: C/r (simplest)
- Growing Perpetuity: C/(r-g) (add growth)
- Annuity: (C/r)[1 - 1/(1+r)^T] (add finite horizon)
- General: No shortcut, calculate term by term

Critical Insight:
These formulas quantify the time value of money for common patterns.
Understanding WHEN to use each formula is as important as knowing the formula.

================================================================================
SECTION 6. NET PRESENT VALUE (NPV)
================================================================================

6.1 FUNDAMENTAL DEFINITION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Net Present Value (NPV) is the present value of ALL cash flows from a project,
including the initial investment.

Formula:
NPV = -I₀ + Σ [CFₜ / (1+r)^t]  for t = 1 to n

Where:
I₀ = initial investment (cash outflow at t=0)
CFₜ = cash flow at time t
r = discount rate (cost of capital)
n = project life

Alternative notation:
NPV = Σ [CFₜ / (1+r)^t]  for t = 0 to n

Where CF₀ = -I₀ (negative because it's an outflow)

Economic Meaning:
NPV measures the INCREASE IN FIRM VALUE from undertaking the project.

NPV > 0: Project creates value → ACCEPT
NPV = 0: Project neither creates nor destroys value → INDIFFERENT
NPV < 0: Project destroys value → REJECT

6.2 THE NPV DECISION RULE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

NPV RULE:
Accept all projects with NPV > 0.
Reject all projects with NPV < 0.

This rule MAXIMIZES firm value.

Why NPV Works:
1. Accounts for time value of money (discounts future cash flows)
2. Accounts for risk (through discount rate)
3. Uses ALL cash flows (not just subset)
4. Is additive (NPV of A+B = NPV(A) + NPV(B))
5. Is consistent with value maximization objective

Mathematical Proof of Value Creation:
If NPV > 0, then:
PV(Cash Inflows) > PV(Cash Outflows)
Benefits > Costs
Therefore: Project increases wealth

6.3 NPV VS. PROJECT RETURN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Key Insight:
NPV measures VALUE CREATED in dollars.
It does NOT measure percentage return.

Example:
Project A: I₀ = $100, PV(CFs) = $150, NPV = $50
Project B: I₀ = $1,000, PV(CFs) = $1,200, NPV = $200

Which is better?
- Project B creates more value ($200 vs. $50)
- Project A has higher return (50% vs. 20%)

If capital is unlimited: Take both (both have NPV > 0)
If capital is limited (can only choose one): Take B (higher NPV)

This is why NPV is superior to percentage-based metrics.

6.4 THE DISCOUNT RATE IN NPV
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The discount rate (r) in NPV represents:
1. The opportunity cost of capital
2. The required return for the project's risk level

How to determine r:
- For firm-level projects: Use WACC
- For divisional projects: Use division-specific WACC
- For projects with different risk: Adjust for risk

Critical Principle:
The discount rate must match the risk of the cash flows.

Impact of Discount Rate on NPV:
- Higher r → Lower NPV (future cash flows worth less)
- Lower r → Higher NPV (future cash flows worth more)

Sensitivity Analysis:
Always test NPV across a range of discount rates.

Example:
Project with I₀ = $1,000 and $300/year for 5 years

At r = 5%: NPV = -$1,000 + $300 × 4.329 = $299
At r = 10%: NPV = -$1,000 + $300 × 3.791 = $137
At r = 15%: NPV = -$1,000 + $300 × 3.352 = $6
At r = 20%: NPV = -$1,000 + $300 × 2.991 = -$103

Notice: As r increases, NPV decreases. At r ≈ 15.2%, NPV = 0 (this is the IRR).

6.5 NPV WITH DIFFERENT CASH FLOW PATTERNS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Pattern 1: Simple Single Investment
CF₀ = -I₀ (outflow)
CF₁ to CFₙ = positive (inflows)

This is the standard case.

Pattern 2: Multiple Investments
CF₀ = -I₀
CF₁ = -I₁ (additional investment)
CF₂ to CFₙ = positive

Example: Construction project with phased investments

Pattern 3: Investment with Salvage Value
CF₀ = -I₀
CF₁ to CFₙ₋₁ = operating cash flows
CFₙ = operating CF + salvage value + working capital recovery

Pattern 4: Replacement Decision
CF₀ = -new equipment cost + salvage from old equipment (after-tax)
CF₁ to CFₙ = incremental cash flows (new minus old)

All patterns use the same NPV formula. Just ensure cash flows are incremental.

6.6 NPV ADDITIVITY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Fundamental Property:
NPV(A + B) = NPV(A) + NPV(B)

This means:
- Firm value = Sum of NPVs of all projects
- Can evaluate projects independently
- Can decompose complex projects into components

Example:
Project A: NPV = $100
Project B: NPV = $150
Project A+B: NPV = $250

Implication:
If both projects are independent and have positive NPV, accept both.

Note on Mutually Exclusive Projects:
When projects are mutually exclusive (can only choose one), pick the one
with highest NPV, not both.

6.7 COMPREHENSIVE EXAMPLE: MANUFACTURING EXPANSION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Scenario:
Company considering expanding manufacturing capacity.

Data:
- Equipment cost: $5,000K (depreciated straight-line over 10 years)
- Installation costs: $500K (also depreciated)
- Initial inventory: $300K (working capital)
- Annual revenue increase: $2,000K
- Annual operating costs increase: $1,200K
- Project life: 10 years
- Salvage value: $1,000K (at end)
- Tax rate: 30%
- WACC: 12%

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate Annual Depreciation
Depreciable basis = $5,000K + $500K = $5,500K
Annual depreciation = $5,500K / 10 = $550K

Step 2: Calculate Annual Operating Cash Flow (Years 1-10)
Revenue: $2,000K
Operating costs: -$1,200K
Depreciation: -$550K
EBIT: $250K
Tax (30%): -$75K
NOPAT: $175K
Add back depreciation: +$550K
Operating Cash Flow: $725K per year

Step 3: Calculate Initial Investment (Year 0)
Equipment: -$5,000K
Installation: -$500K
Working capital: -$300K
Total: -$5,800K

Step 4: Calculate Terminal Cash Flow (Year 10)
Operating CF: $725K
Salvage value (after-tax): $1,000K × (1 - 0.30) = $700K
Working capital recovery: $300K
Total Year 10 CF: $1,725K

Step 5: Calculate NPV
NPV = -$5,800K + $725K × PVAF(12%, 9) + $1,725K / (1.12)^10

Where PVAF(12%, 9) = annuity factor for years 1-9
PVAF = [1 - 1/(1.12)^9] / 0.12 = 5.328

NPV = -$5,800K + $725K × 5.328 + $1,725K / 3.106
NPV = -$5,800K + $3,863K + $555K
NPV = -$1,382K

Decision: REJECT (NPV < 0)

The project destroys value despite positive cash flows because the return
(which would be about 7-8%) is below the cost of capital (12%).

6.8 COMMON STUDENT MISTAKES WITH NPV
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

MISTAKE 1: Using wrong cash flows
- Including sunk costs
- Forgetting opportunity costs
- Mixing accounting profit with cash flow

MISTAKE 2: Wrong discount rate
- Using promised interest rate instead of expected return
- Using same rate for different risk projects
- Not adjusting for project-specific risk

MISTAKE 3: Sign errors
- Forgetting that initial investment is negative
- Incorrect signs on working capital changes

MISTAKE 4: Timing errors
- Discounting Year 0 cash flows (they're already in PV)
- Off-by-one errors in period counting

MISTAKE 5: Forgetting terminal flows
- Omitting salvage value
- Forgetting working capital recovery

MISTAKE 6: Inconsistent assumptions
- Nominal CFs with real discount rate
- Pre-tax CFs with after-tax discount rate

6.9 NPV STRENGTHS AND LIMITATIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

STRENGTHS:
✓ Theoretically correct (measures value creation)
✓ Accounts for time value of money
✓ Accounts for all cash flows
✓ Accounts for risk (through discount rate)
✓ Additive property
✓ Consistent with value maximization
✓ Works with any cash flow pattern

LIMITATIONS:
✗ Requires estimate of discount rate (which can be uncertain)
✗ Requires cash flow forecasts (which may be inaccurate)
✗ Doesn't consider flexibility or real options
✗ Hard to communicate (dollar value vs. return percentage)
✗ Scale-dependent (favors large projects over high-return small projects)

Despite limitations, NPV is the GOLD STANDARD for project evaluation.

6.10 TEACHING NOTES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
When teaching NPV:
1. Start with simple examples (one initial outflow, constant inflows)
2. Gradually add complexity (varying cash flows, terminal values, taxes)
3. Emphasize that NPV measures VALUE CREATED, not return
4. Work through complete examples with all components
5. Show sensitivity to discount rate
6. Compare to alternative metrics (covered in next sections)
7. Stress that NPV should never be overridden by other rules

Key Teaching Moment:
Many students want to use IRR because it's a percentage. Explain that while
IRR is intuitive, NPV is more reliable and should always take precedence when
they conflict.

Practice Exercise Types:
- Calculate NPV from given cash flows
- Construct cash flows from financial data
- Determine breakeven values (what revenue/cost makes NPV = 0?)
- Compare mutually exclusive projects
- Evaluate sensitivity to assumptions

================================================================================
SECTION 7. INTERNAL RATE OF RETURN (IRR)
================================================================================

7.1 DEFINITION AND CONCEPT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Internal Rate of Return (IRR) is the discount rate that makes NPV equal to zero.

Mathematical Definition:
IRR is the value of r that solves:
0 = -I₀ + Σ [CFₜ / (1+r)^t]

Or equivalently:
I₀ = Σ [CFₜ / (1+IRR)^t]

Economic Interpretation:
IRR is the "break-even" return on the project. It's the rate at which:
PV(Cash Inflows) = PV(Cash Outflows)

Alternative names:
- Yield to maturity (for bonds)
- Money-weighted return (for portfolios)
- Internal rate of return (for projects)

7.2 THE IRR DECISION RULE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

IRR RULE:
Accept project if IRR > required return (cost of capital)
Reject project if IRR < required return

Equivalently:
Accept if IRR > r
Reject if IRR < r

Where r = discount rate (WACC or required return)

Why This Makes Sense:
If IRR > r: The project earns more than its cost of capital → value creation
If IRR < r: The project earns less than its cost of capital → value destruction

7.3 RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN NPV AND IRR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Key Relationship:
For standard cash flows (one sign change):
- If IRR > r, then NPV > 0 (accept)
- If IRR < r, then NPV < 0 (reject)
- If IRR = r, then NPV = 0 (indifferent)

Graphically:
NPV decreases as discount rate increases.
The IRR is where the NPV curve crosses zero.

      NPV
       |
       |    \
       |      \
       |        \
     0 |----------\---------> discount rate
       |            \  IRR
       |              \
       |

Example:
Project: I₀ = -$1,000, CF₁ = $300, CF₂ = $400, CF₃ = $500

At r = 0%: NPV = -$1,000 + $1,200 = $200
At r = 5%: NPV = -$1,000 + $286 + $363 + $432 = $81
At r = 10%: NPV = -$1,000 + $273 + $331 + $376 = -$20
IRR ≈ 9.7% (between 5% and 10%)

7.4 CALCULATING IRR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Methods:

METHOD 1: Trial and Error
1. Try different discount rates
2. Calculate NPV for each
3. Narrow down until NPV ≈ 0

METHOD 2: Financial Calculator
Use built-in IRR function with cash flows.

METHOD 3: Excel
Use =IRR(values, [guess]) function
Example: =IRR(A1:A11) where A1:A11 contains cash flows from period 0 to 10

METHOD 4: Interpolation
If NPV(r₁) > 0 and NPV(r₂) < 0, then r₁ < IRR < r₂

Linear interpolation:
IRR ≈ r₁ + [NPV(r₁) / (NPV(r₁) - NPV(r₂))] × (r₂ - r₁)

METHOD 5: Solver (for complex cases)
Use Excel Solver or similar optimization tools.

Practical Note:
In practice, always use Excel or financial calculator. Trial and error is
for understanding, not for actual calculations.

7.5 WHY IRR IS POPULAR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

REASON 1: Intuitive Communication
"This project earns 25%" is easier to understand than "NPV = $432K"

REASON 2: No Discount Rate Needed (Initially)
Can calculate IRR without knowing required return, then compare.

REASON 3: Comparable Across Sizes
25% return sounds comparable whether project is $100K or $100M.

REASON 4: Easy to Compare
IRR > WACC is simple rule.

REASON 5: Related to Return Metrics
Similar to ROI, ROE, and other return measures managers know.

Despite popularity, IRR has serious problems (covered next).

7.6 PROBLEMS WITH IRR: MULTIPLE IRRs
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROBLEM:
When cash flows change sign more than once, there can be multiple IRRs.

Example (Famous Problem):
CF₀ = -$1,000
CF₁ = +$5,000
CF₂ = -$6,000

Solving for IRR:
0 = -1,000 + 5,000/(1+r) - 6,000/(1+r)²

This quadratic equation has TWO solutions:
IRR₁ = 25%
IRR₂ = 200%

Which one to use? Both make NPV = 0!

Why Multiple IRRs Occur:
Mathematically: Polynomial equations can have multiple roots
Economically: Multiple periods with net outflows

Real-World Example:
Mining project with:
- Initial investment (outflow)
- Operating cash flows (inflows)
- Environmental cleanup costs (outflow)

Solution:
Use NPV instead. NPV is always unique.

7.7 PROBLEMS WITH IRR: NO IRR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROBLEM:
Sometimes no IRR exists (no discount rate makes NPV = 0).

Example:
CF₀ = +$1,000 (receive money first)
CF₁ = -$900 (pay back less later)

This has positive NPV at ANY discount rate!
There is no IRR (mathematically, it's complex/imaginary).

Why No IRR:
Cash flow pattern doesn't follow standard investment structure.

Real-World Example:
- Supplier gives you inventory upfront (inflow)
- You pay later (outflow)
- You pay less than you received (time value benefit)

Solution:
Use NPV. IRR rule doesn't work here.

7.8 PROBLEMS WITH IRR: MUTUALLY EXCLUSIVE PROJECTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROBLEM:
IRR can rank mutually exclusive projects incorrectly.

Example:
Project A: I₀ = -$100, CF₁ = $150, IRR = 50%, NPV(10%) = $36
Project B: I₀ = -$100, CF₁ = $60, CF₂ = $60, IRR = 20%, NPV(10%) = $4

If r = 10% (cost of capital):
IRR says: Choose A (50% > 20%)
NPV says: Choose A ($36 > $4)
Conclusion: Both agree, no problem here.

But now consider:
Project C: I₀ = -$100, CF₁ = $130, IRR = 30%, NPV(10%) = $18
Project D: I₀ = -$500, CF₁ = $600, IRR = 20%, NPV(10%) = $45

If r = 10%:
IRR says: Choose C (30% > 20%)
NPV says: Choose D ($45 > $18)

CONFLICT! Which is right?

Answer: NPV is right. Choose D.

Why IRR Fails:
IRR ignores scale. Project D creates more value even though return is lower.

Fisher Separation:
The discount rate at which NPV(C) = NPV(D) is called the crossover rate.
Below this rate: D has higher NPV
Above this rate: C has higher NPV

IRR doesn't account for this relationship.

7.9 PROBLEMS WITH IRR: SCALE PROBLEM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROBLEM:
IRR favors small high-return projects over large moderate-return projects.

Example:
Project Small: I₀ = -$10K, NPV = $5K, IRR = 50%
Project Large: I₀ = -$1,000K, NPV = $200K, IRR = 25%

If capital is limited to choosing one:
IRR says: Choose Small (50% > 25%)
NPV says: Choose Large ($200K > $5K)

Which creates more value? Large project by far.

Analogy:
Would you rather earn:
- 50% return on $10? Profit = $5
- 20% return on $1,000? Profit = $200

Obviously the second option, even though return is lower.

Conclusion:
When comparing projects of different scales, use NPV, not IRR.

7.10 PROBLEMS WITH IRR: TIMING PROBLEM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROBLEM:
IRR doesn't account for when cash flows occur (except through discounting).

Example:
Project Early: CF₀ = -$100, CF₁ = $120, IRR = 20%
Project Late: CF₀ = -$100, CF₁₀ = $619, IRR = 20%

Both have IRR = 20%, but are they equivalent?

At r = 10%:
NPV(Early) = -$100 + $120/1.10 = $9
NPV(Late) = -$100 + $619/(1.10)^10 = $139

NPV(Late) is much better! But IRR doesn't show this.

Why:
IRR implicitly assumes reinvestment at the IRR rate, which may not be realistic.

7.11 MODIFIED IRR (MIRR)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Modified IRR attempts to address some IRR problems.

Calculation:
1. Find terminal value (TV) of all cash inflows, compounded at cost of capital
2. Find present value (PV) of all cash outflows, discounted at cost of capital
3. MIRR is the rate that equates PV of outflows to TV of inflows:

PV(Outflows) × (1 + MIRR)^n = TV(Inflows)

MIRR = [TV(Inflows) / PV(Outflows)]^(1/n) - 1

Advantages:
- Single unique solution
- Assumes reinvestment at cost of capital (more realistic)
- Can handle non-standard cash flows

Disadvantages:
- Less intuitive than IRR
- Not as widely used
- Still inferior to NPV for decision-making

Bottom Line:
MIRR is better than IRR, but NPV is better than both.

7.12 IRR VS. NPV: WHEN THEY CONFLICT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

General Rule:
When IRR and NPV give different recommendations, ALWAYS follow NPV.

Situations Where Conflict Can Occur:
1. Mutually exclusive projects
2. Different project scales
3. Different cash flow timing
4. Non-standard cash flows (multiple sign changes)

Why NPV Wins:
- Measures value creation directly
- No ambiguity
- Always provides clear answer
- Consistent with wealth maximization

When IRR and NPV Agree:
- Independent projects (accept all with NPV > 0 and IRR > r)
- Standard cash flows (single sign change)
- No capital constraints
- Same scale projects

7.13 PRACTICAL USE OF IRR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Despite problems, IRR remains widely used in practice.

Appropriate Uses:
1. Quick screening of independent projects
2. Communication to non-finance stakeholders
3. Comparing to hurdle rates
4. Benchmarking against other investments
5. Sensitivity analysis (how returns change with assumptions)

Best Practice:
- Calculate BOTH NPV and IRR
- Use NPV for final decision
- Use IRR for communication and context
- Be aware of IRR's limitations
- If IRR seems strange, check for multiple IRRs or other issues

Real-World Example:
"This factory expansion has NPV of $50M and IRR of 18%, well above our 12%
hurdle rate." (Uses both metrics effectively)

7.14 COMPREHENSIVE EXAMPLE: NPV VS. IRR CONFLICT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Scenario:
Two mutually exclusive projects. Choose one.

Project X:
CF₀ = -$1,000
CF₁ = $500
CF₂ = $500
CF₃ = $500

Project Y:
CF₀ = -$1,000
CF₁ = $100
CF₂ = $100
CF₃ = $1,500

Cost of capital: 10%

Solution:

Calculate IRR for Project X:
0 = -$1,000 + $500/(1+r) + $500/(1+r)² + $500/(1+r)³
This is an annuity: $1,000 = $500 × [1 - 1/(1+r)³] / r
Solving: IRR(X) ≈ 23.4%

Calculate IRR for Project Y:
0 = -$1,000 + $100/(1+r) + $100/(1+r)² + $1,500/(1+r)³
Solving numerically: IRR(Y) ≈ 19.7%

IRR says: Choose Project X (23.4% > 19.7%)

Calculate NPV for Project X at 10%:
NPV(X) = -$1,000 + $500 × 2.487 = -$1,000 + $1,244 = $244

Calculate NPV for Project Y at 10%:
NPV(Y) = -$1,000 + $100/1.10 + $100/1.21 + $1,500/1.331
NPV(Y) = -$1,000 + $91 + $83 + $1,127 = $301

NPV says: Choose Project Y ($301 > $244)

CONFLICT!

Resolution:
Follow NPV. Choose Project Y.
It creates more value ($301 vs. $244) even though return is lower (19.7% vs. 23.4%).

Intuition:
Project Y has larger cash flow in Year 3, which is worth more in PV terms
than the earlier cash flows of Project X, given the 10% cost of capital.

7.15 COMMON STUDENT MISCONCEPTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

MISCONCEPTION 1: "IRR is the average return per year"
REALITY: IRR is a complex average that accounts for timing and compounding.
It's not a simple arithmetic average.

MISCONCEPTION 2: "Higher IRR always means better project"
REALITY: Higher IRR can be misleading when comparing projects of different
scales or timing. NPV is the reliable metric.

MISCONCEPTION 3: "If IRR > WACC, NPV must be positive"
REALITY: True for standard cash flows. False if multiple IRRs exist.

MISCONCEPTION 4: "IRR tells you how much money you'll make"
REALITY: IRR is a percentage return, not a dollar amount. It doesn't indicate
value created.

MISCONCEPTION 5: "We should always maximize IRR"
REALITY: We should maximize value (NPV), not return (IRR).

7.16 TEACHING NOTES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
When teaching IRR:
1. Start by explaining it's where NPV = 0 (crossover point)
2. Show graphically how IRR relates to NPV curve
3. Demonstrate calculation using Excel
4. Present simple examples where IRR = NPV agree
5. Then show examples where they conflict
6. Emphasize that NPV should always win conflicts
7. Explain why IRR is still used (communication, intuition)
8. Practice identifying situations with multiple IRRs

Key Teaching Moment:
Students often prefer IRR because percentages are familiar. Must convince them
that dollars of value creation (NPV) matter more than percentage returns (IRR).

Use analogies:
"Would you rather have 100% return on $1 (earn $1) or 10% return on $1,000
(earn $100)?"

Critical Insight:
IRR is not "wrong" - it's just less reliable than NPV. Use both, but when
they conflict, trust NPV.

================================================================================
SECTION 8. PAYBACK PERIOD
================================================================================

8.1 DEFINITION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Payback Period is the time required to recover the initial investment from
project cash flows.

Formula:
Find T such that: Σ CFₜ ≥ I₀  for t = 1 to T

In words:
How many years until cumulative cash flows equal initial investment?

Example:
I₀ = -$1,000
CF₁ = $300
CF₂ = $400
CF₃ = $500

Cumulative CFs:
Year 1: $300
Year 2: $700 ($300 + $400)
Year 3: $1,200 ($700 + $500)

Payback occurs between Year 2 and Year 3.
More precisely: 2 + ($1,000 - $700)/$500 = 2.6 years

8.2 PAYBACK DECISION RULE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PAYBACK RULE:
Accept if payback period < cutoff period
Reject if payback period > cutoff period

Where cutoff is subjectively determined by management.

Common Cutoffs:
- Tech startups: 2-3 years
- Manufacturing: 3-5 years
- Real estate: 5-10 years
- Infrastructure: 10-20 years

Rationale:
Managers want to recover investment quickly to reduce risk.

8.3 WHY PAYBACK IS USED
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

REASON 1: Simplicity
Easy to calculate and understand.

REASON 2: Liquidity Measure
Indicates how quickly cash is recovered.

REASON 3: Risk Proxy
Shorter payback = less exposure to uncertainty.

REASON 4: Practical in Uncertain Environments
When distant future is very uncertain, focus on near-term cash flows.

REASON 5: Quick Screening
Eliminates clearly bad projects efficiently.

REASON 6: Communication
Easy to explain to non-finance stakeholders.

8.4 PROBLEMS WITH PAYBACK
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PROBLEM 1: Ignores Time Value of Money
Treats $100 in Year 1 the same as $100 in Year 5.

Example:
Project A: CF = $500/year for 2 years (payback = 2 years)
Project B: CF = $1,000 in Year 1, $0 thereafter (payback = 1 year)

Payback says B is better (shorter payback).
But if Year 2 cash flows are discounted, comparison changes.

PROBLEM 2: Ignores Cash Flows After Payback
Doesn't consider what happens after investment is recovered.

Example:
Project A: I₀ = -$1,000, CF = $500/year for 3 years
Project B: I₀ = -$1,000, CF = $500/year for 10 years

Both have payback = 2 years.
Payback says they're equivalent.
But Project B is clearly better (7 extra years of cash flows)!

PROBLEM 3: Arbitrary Cutoff
No economic logic for choosing cutoff period.

Why 3 years and not 4? No clear answer.

PROBLEM 4: Inconsistent with Value Maximization
Can accept negative NPV projects or reject positive NPV projects.

Example:
Project: I₀ = -$1,000, CF₁ = $1,200
Payback = 0.83 years ✓ (very quick)
But if r = 25%: NPV = -$1,000 + $1,200/1.25 = -$40 ✗ (negative!)

Should accept based on payback, but destroys value!

PROBLEM 5: Biases Against Long-Term Projects
Discriminates against R&D, infrastructure, strategic investments.

These often have long paybacks but high NPVs.

8.5 DISCOUNTED PAYBACK PERIOD
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Discounted Payback addresses Problem 1 (ignores time value of money).

Definition:
Time required to recover initial investment from DISCOUNTED cash flows.

Formula:
Find T such that: Σ [CFₜ / (1+r)^t] ≥ I₀  for t = 1 to T

Example:
I₀ = -$1,000, r = 10%
CF₁ = $300, CF₂ = $400, CF₃ = $500

Discounted CFs:
Year 1: $300/1.10 = $273
Year 2: $400/1.21 = $331
Year 3: $500/1.331 = $376

Cumulative discounted CFs:
Year 1: $273
Year 2: $604 ($273 + $331)
Year 3: $980 ($604 + $376)

Never fully recovers $1,000 in PV terms → Discounted payback > 3 years

Advantages Over Regular Payback:
✓ Accounts for time value of money
✓ More conservative

Remaining Problems:
✗ Still ignores cash flows after payback
✗ Still uses arbitrary cutoff
✗ Still not consistent with NPV

Bottom Line:
Discounted payback is better than regular payback but still inferior to NPV.

8.6 WHEN PAYBACK MIGHT BE APPROPRIATE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Situations Where Payback Can Be Useful:

SITUATION 1: Very High Uncertainty
When long-term forecasts are unreliable, focusing on near-term recovery
makes sense.

Example: Technology startups in rapidly changing markets

SITUATION 2: Capital Rationing
When company has limited access to capital, quick recovery allows
reinvestment.

SITUATION 3: Initial Screening
Use as first pass to eliminate obviously bad projects before detailed analysis.

SITUATION 4: Complement to NPV
Report both metrics: "NPV is $5M with payback of 3 years."

Best Practice:
Use payback as a secondary metric or constraint, never as primary decision rule.

Example:
"Accept all projects with NPV > 0 AND payback < 5 years."

This uses NPV for value assessment and payback as risk constraint.

8.7 COMPREHENSIVE EXAMPLE: PAYBACK VS. NPV
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Scenario:
Three mutually exclusive projects. Cost of capital = 12%.

Project A:
CF₀ = -$1,000
CF₁ to CF₃ = $400 per year

Project B:
CF₀ = -$1,000
CF₁ = $900
CF₂ = $200

Project C:
CF₀ = -$1,000
CF₁ = $100
CF₂ = $200
CF₃ to CF₁₀ = $200 per year

Analysis:

PAYBACK PERIODS:
Project A: 2.5 years ($400 × 2.5 = $1,000)
Project B: 1.5 years ($900 + $100 = $1,000 after 1.5 years)
Project C: 3.5 years ($100 + $200 + $200×1.5 = $1,000)

Payback ranking: B (best), A, C (worst)

NPV at 12%:
Project A: -$1,000 + $400 × 2.402 = -$1,000 + $961 = -$39
Project B: -$1,000 + $900/1.12 + $200/1.25 = -$1,000 + $804 + $160 = -$36
Project C: -$1,000 + $100/1.12 + $200/1.25 + $200 × 5.328 / 1.25
         = -$1,000 + $89 + $160 + $852 = $101

NPV ranking: C (best, only positive), B, A (worst)

Conclusion:
Payback says choose B.
NPV says choose C (only one with positive NPV).

Following payback would reject the only value-creating project!

Intuition:
Project C has long life (10 years) with steady cash flows. High NPV despite
longer payback. Projects A and B have negative NPV because returns don't
exceed cost of capital.

8.8 COMMON STUDENT MISCONCEPTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

MISCONCEPTION 1: "Shorter payback always means better project"
REALITY: A project with 1-year payback and negative NPV destroys value.
Payback doesn't measure value creation.

MISCONCEPTION 2: "Discounted payback is equivalent to NPV"
REALITY: Discounted payback still ignores cash flows after the payback period.

MISCONCEPTION 3: "Companies that use payback are unsophisticated"
REALITY: Many sophisticated companies use payback as a secondary criterion
or risk measure, not as the primary decision rule.

MISCONCEPTION 4: "Payback measures project return"
REALITY: Payback measures time to recovery, not return or profitability.

8.9 TEACHING NOTES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
When teaching payback:
1. Start by explaining the intuitive appeal
2. Show how to calculate it (very simple)
3. Then systematically introduce each problem
4. Use examples where payback gives wrong answer
5. Introduce discounted payback as partial improvement
6. Emphasize it can complement NPV but should never override it
7. Discuss real-world contexts where it's used

Key Teaching Moment:
Students often think payback is "obviously bad." Explain that it captures
something real (speed of recovery, liquidity) but captures it imperfectly.
It's not useless, just inferior to NPV.

Example for Class Discussion:
"Your startup has 2 years of runway. Should you use payback period? Why or
why not?" (Answer: Yes as a constraint, but still maximize NPV subject to
that constraint.)

================================================================================
SECTION 9. PROFITABILITY INDEX (PI)
================================================================================

9.1 DEFINITION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Profitability Index (PI), also called Benefit-Cost Ratio, measures value
created per dollar invested.

Formula:
PI = PV(Cash Inflows) / PV(Cash Outflows)

Or equivalently:
PI = PV(Cash Inflows) / Initial Investment

Or in terms of NPV:
PI = [Initial Investment + NPV] / Initial Investment
PI = 1 + (NPV / Initial Investment)

Example:
Project: I₀ = -$1,000, PV(future CFs) = $1,300
PI = $1,300 / $1,000 = 1.30

Interpretation:
For every $1 invested, we get back $1.30 in present value terms.
Net gain: $0.30 per dollar invested.

9.2 PI DECISION RULE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

PI RULE:
Accept if PI > 1
Reject if PI < 1

Relationship to NPV:
- PI > 1 ⟺ PV(Inflows) > PV(Outflows) ⟺ NPV > 0 ✓ Accept
- PI < 1 ⟺ PV(Inflows) < PV(Outflows) ⟺ NPV < 0 ✗ Reject
- PI = 1 ⟺ NPV = 0 (breakeven)

Why PI = 1 is the cutoff:
PI = 1 means PV(Inflows) = PV(Outflows), so NPV = 0.

Advantage of PI:
Normalizes for project size. Useful when comparing projects requiring
different investments.

9.3 PI VS. NPV FOR INDEPENDENT PROJECTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

For INDEPENDENT projects (can accept multiple), PI and NPV always agree.

Proof:
PI > 1 ⟺ PV(Inflows)/PV(Outflows) > 1
       ⟺ PV(Inflows) > PV(Outflows)
       ⟺ NPV > 0

Therefore:
- All projects with PI > 1 also have NPV > 0 → Accept both
- All projects with PI < 1 also have NPV < 0 → Reject both

No conflict when projects are independent.

9.4 PI VS. NPV FOR MUTUALLY EXCLUSIVE PROJECTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

For MUTUALLY EXCLUSIVE projects (can only choose one), PI and NPV can conflict.

Example:
Project A: I₀ = -$100, PV(CFs) = $150, NPV = $50, PI = 1.50
Project B: I₀ = -$1,000, PV(CFs) = $1,400, NPV = $400, PI = 1.40

PI says: Choose A (1.50 > 1.40)
NPV says: Choose B ($400 > $50)

Which is correct?

Answer: NPV is correct. Choose B.

Why NPV Wins:
Project B creates more absolute value ($400 vs. $50).
PI focuses on relative return (per dollar), not absolute value creation.

Analogy:
Would you rather have:
- 50% return on $100 (earn $50)
- 40% return on $1,000 (earn $400)

Obviously the second, even though return per dollar is lower.

9.5 CAPITAL RATIONING AND PI
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Capital Rationing:
When a firm cannot invest in all positive NPV projects due to limited capital.

In this case, PI can be useful for RANKING projects.

Strategy Under Capital Rationing:
1. Calculate PI for all projects
2. Rank projects by PI (highest first)
3. Accept projects in order until capital is exhausted

Why This Works:
Maximizes value per dollar of limited capital.

Example:
Available capital: $1,000
Projects:
A: I₀ = -$400, NPV = $200, PI = 1.50
B: I₀ = -$300, NPV = $120, PI = 1.40
C: I₀ = -$500, NPV = $200, PI = 1.40
D: I₀ = -$600, NPV = $180, PI = 1.30

PI ranking: A, B, C, D

Choose: A ($400) + B ($300) + part of C (only $300 available)
Total NPV: $200 + $120 + $120 (60% of C's NPV) = $440

This is better than choosing largest NPV projects (A + C: NPV = $400).

Limitations:
- Capital rationing itself is questionable (why not raise more capital if
  projects have positive NPV?)
- Doesn't account for divisibility (some projects can't be partially funded)
- Doesn't account for multi-period capital constraints

9.6 PI WITH MULTIPLE OUTFLOWS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

When project has outflows in multiple periods:

Formula:
PI = PV(All Inflows) / PV(All Outflows)

Where outflows are discounted to time 0.

Example:
CF₀ = -$1,000 (outflow)
CF₁ = -$200 (additional investment, outflow)
CF₂ = $800 (inflow)
CF₃ = $800 (inflow)
r = 10%

PV(Outflows) = $1,000 + $200/1.10 = $1,000 + $182 = $1,182
PV(Inflows) = $800/1.21 + $800/1.331 = $661 + $601 = $1,262
PI = $1,262 / $1,182 = 1.07 ✓ (Accept, PI > 1)

NPV = $1,262 - $1,182 = $80 ✓ (Confirms: NPV > 0)

9.7 STRENGTHS AND WEAKNESSES OF PI
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

STRENGTHS:
✓ Easy to interpret (bang for buck)
✓ Accounts for time value of money
✓ Consistent with NPV for independent projects
✓ Useful for ranking under capital rationing
✓ Normalizes for project size

WEAKNESSES:
✗ Can conflict with NPV for mutually exclusive projects
✗ Doesn't measure absolute value creation
✗ Can be misleading when comparing different scales
✗ Less commonly used than NPV or IRR in practice
✗ Requires careful handling of multiple outflows

Bottom Line:
PI is useful as a supplementary metric, especially for capital rationing, but
NPV should be the primary decision criterion.

9.8 COMPREHENSIVE EXAMPLE: PI APPLICATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Scenario:
Company evaluating 4 projects. Total capital available: $2,000K.
Cost of capital: 12%.

Project A:
CF₀ = -$800K
CF₁ to CF₅ = $300K per year
PV(CFs at 12%) = $300K × 3.605 = $1,082K
NPV = $1,082K - $800K = $282K
PI = $1,082K / $800K = 1.35

Project B:
CF₀ = -$600K
CF₁ to CF₅ = $250K per year
PV(CFs at 12%) = $250K × 3.605 = $901K
NPV = $901K - $600K = $301K
PI = $901K / $600K = 1.50

Project C:
CF₀ = -$1,000K
CF₁ to CF₅ = $350K per year
PV(CFs at 12%) = $350K × 3.605 = $1,262K
NPV = $1,262K - $1,000K = $262K
PI = $1,262K / $1,000K = 1.26

Project D:
CF₀ = -$700K
CF₁ to CF₅ = $280K per year
PV(CFs at 12%) = $280K × 3.605 = $1,009K
NPV = $1,009K - $700K = $309K
PI = $1,009K / $700K = 1.44

Analysis:

WITHOUT Capital Constraint:
All projects have PI > 1 and NPV > 0 → Accept all
Total investment: $3,100K (exceeds available $2,000K)
Total NPV: $282K + $301K + $262K + $309K = $1,154K

WITH Capital Constraint ($2,000K):

Ranking by NPV (highest first):
1. D: NPV = $309K
2. B: NPV = $301K
3. A: NPV = $282K
4. C: NPV = $262K

Choose: D ($700K) + B ($600K) + A ($800K) = $2,100K (exceeds budget)
Actually choose: D + B + partial A? Or D + B only?
D + B: Investment = $1,300K, NPV = $610K

Ranking by PI (highest first):
1. B: PI = 1.50
2. D: PI = 1.44
3. A: PI = 1.35
4. C: PI = 1.26

Choose: B ($600K) + D ($700K) + A ($800K) = $2,100K (exceeds budget)
Actually choose: B + D = $1,300K, NPV = $610K
Or: B + D + part of A (with $700K) = NPV = $610K + $247K = $857K

PI approach gives better result: $857K vs. $610K

This demonstrates PI's value under capital rationing.

9.9 COMMON STUDENT MISCONCEPTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

MISCONCEPTION 1: "PI and NPV always give the same answer"
REALITY: They agree for independent projects but can conflict for mutually
exclusive projects.

MISCONCEPTION 2: "Higher PI always means better project"
REALITY: For mutually exclusive projects, highest NPV project may not have
highest PI. Follow NPV.

MISCONCEPTION 3: "PI measures return"
REALITY: PI measures value per dollar invested, not return over time (that's IRR).

MISCONCEPTION 4: "PI = 2 means 100% return"
REALITY: PI = 2 means $2 in PV per $1 invested, which is $1 net gain per $1
invested. But this is in PV terms, not an annual return.

9.10 TEACHING NOTES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
When teaching PI:
1. Explain it as NPV per dollar invested
2. Show that PI > 1 ⟺ NPV > 0
3. Demonstrate calculation from cash flows
4. Show agreement with NPV for independent projects
5. Show potential conflict for mutually exclusive projects
6. Explain use case: capital rationing
7. Practice ranking exercises

Key Teaching Moment:
Students may wonder why we need PI if we have NPV. Explain:
- Under unlimited capital, NPV is sufficient
- Under capital rationing, PI helps rank projects
- PI is intuitive (bang for buck)
- But NPV should dominate for final decisions

Useful Class Exercise:
Give students capital rationing problem. Have them solve using:
1. NPV ranking
2. PI ranking
Compare results. Show PI gives better outcome under constraint.

================================================================================
SECTION 10. COMPARING DECISION RULES - SUMMARY
================================================================================

10.1 DECISION RULES COMPARISON TABLE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

| Metric   | Formula                    | Decision Rule      | Advantages | Disadvantages |
|----------|----------------------------|--------------------|------------|---------------|
| NPV      | Σ CFₜ/(1+r)^t - I₀         | Accept if NPV > 0  | Theoretically correct, measures value creation, works with any cash flow pattern | Requires discount rate estimate, dollar amount not % |
| IRR      | Solve for r: NPV = 0       | Accept if IRR > r  | Intuitive (%), no discount rate needed initially | Multiple/no IRR, conflicts with NPV, ignores scale |
| Payback  | Years to recover I₀        | Accept if < cutoff | Simple, measures liquidity/risk | Ignores TVM, ignores CFs after payback, arbitrary cutoff |
| Disc PB  | Years to recover in PV     | Accept if < cutoff | Accounts for TVM | Still ignores CFs after payback, arbitrary cutoff |
| PI       | PV(Inflows)/PV(Outflows)   | Accept if PI > 1   | Normalizes for size, useful under capital rationing | Can conflict with NPV, doesn't measure absolute value |

10.2 WHICH RULE TO USE? DECISION FLOWCHART
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

START: Do you want to maximize firm value?
│
YES → Use NPV as PRIMARY decision criterion
│
├─ Calculate NPV
│
├─ Is NPV > 0?
│   │
│   ├─ YES → ACCEPT project (creates value)
│   │
│   └─ NO → REJECT project (destroys value)
│
└─ Optional: Calculate supplementary metrics for additional insight
    │
    ├─ IRR: For communication and comparing to benchmarks
    │
    ├─ Payback: As risk/liquidity check
    │
    └─ PI: If capital rationing or need to rank projects

Special Cases:
1. Capital rationing → Use PI to rank, then NPV to confirm
2. Very high uncertainty → Payback as additional constraint
3. Mutually exclusive projects → Always use NPV to choose
4. Non-standard cash flows → Use only NPV (IRR unreliable)

10.3 WHEN METRICS AGREE VS. DISAGREE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WHEN ALL METRICS AGREE:
- Independent project with standard cash flows (single sign change)
- NPV > 0, IRR > r, Payback < cutoff, PI > 1
- Decision is clear: ACCEPT

WHEN METRICS DISAGREE:
Common conflicts:
1. NPV vs. IRR (mutually exclusive projects of different scales)
2. NPV vs. Payback (long-term project with positive NPV but long payback)
3. NPV vs. PI (large project with lower PI but higher NPV)

Resolution Rule:
ALWAYS follow NPV when metrics conflict.

Why:
NPV directly measures value creation, which is the firm's objective.
Other metrics capture important aspects but can be misleading.

10.4 REAL-WORLD PRACTICE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Survey Evidence:
Most firms use MULTIPLE metrics:
- 75% use NPV
- 75% use IRR
- 57% use Payback
- 12% use PI
(Graham & Harvey survey of CFOs)

Interpretation:
- NPV and IRR are most common
- Used together (not substitutes)
- Payback as supplementary criterion
- PI rarely used except in specific situations

Best Practice in Industry:
1. Calculate NPV (primary decision criterion)
2. Calculate IRR (for communication and context)
3. Report payback (as risk/liquidity measure)
4. Present all three to management
5. Make final decision based on NPV
6. Use IRR and payback to explain the decision

Example Management Report:
"We recommend accepting Project Alpha. It has:
- NPV of $15.3M (creates significant value)
- IRR of 22% (well above our 12% hurdle rate)
- Payback of 3.2 years (acceptable risk profile)
All metrics support acceptance."

10.5 TEACHING FRAMEWORK: HIERARCHY OF METRICS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Think of decision rules as a hierarchy:

TIER 1 (Gold Standard):
- NPV: Theoretically correct, always reliable
- Use for: Final decisions

TIER 2 (Useful Complements):
- IRR: Good for communication, can conflict with NPV
- Use for: Context, benchmarking

TIER 3 (Rough Screens):
- Discounted Payback: Better than regular payback
- PI: Useful for capital rationing
- Use for: Initial screening, special situations

TIER 4 (Seriously Flawed):
- Regular Payback: Many problems, very limited use
- Use for: Very rough liquidity check only

Teaching Principle:
Teach NPV first and thoroughly.
Then introduce others as complements or alternatives, explaining their
limitations relative to NPV.

10.6 COMMON STUDENT MISCONCEPTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

MISCONCEPTION 1: "Different metrics are substitutes - pick your favorite"
REALITY: NPV is theoretically superior. Others are complements, not substitutes.

MISCONCEPTION 2: "If three metrics say accept and one says reject, go with majority"
REALITY: If NPV says reject, reject. NPV should dominate.

MISCONCEPTION 3: "Companies use the 'best' metric"
REALITY: Companies use multiple metrics because each provides different
information. IRR for communication, payback for risk, NPV for decisions.

MISCONCEPTION 4: "More sophisticated firms only use NPV"
REALITY: Even sophisticated firms use IRR and payback as supplementary tools.

10.7 PRACTICE PROBLEMS FOR STUDENTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Problem Type 1: Independent Projects
Given: Cash flows and discount rate
Task: Calculate NPV, IRR, Payback, PI
Question: Which projects should be accepted?
Learning: When metrics agree (all positive NPV projects accepted)

Problem Type 2: Mutually Exclusive Projects
Given: Cash flows for two projects
Task: Calculate all metrics, identify conflicts
Question: Which project should be chosen?
Learning: NPV wins conflicts

Problem Type 3: Non-Standard Cash Flows
Given: Project with multiple sign changes
Task: Attempt to find IRR
Learning: IRR may not exist or multiple IRRs may exist

Problem Type 4: Capital Rationing
Given: Multiple projects, limited budget
Task: Rank by NPV vs. PI
Question: Which combination maximizes value?
Learning: PI useful for ranking under constraints

Problem Type 5: Sensitivity Analysis
Given: Base case assumptions
Task: Calculate NPV under different scenarios
Question: How robust is the decision?
Learning: All metrics depend on assumptions

10.8 TEACHING NOTES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
When teaching decision rules comparison:
1. Present NPV first as the benchmark
2. Introduce each alternative, explaining its appeal
3. Systematically show each alternative's limitations
4. Use examples where different metrics conflict
5. Always resolve conflicts in favor of NPV
6. Explain why multiple metrics are used in practice
7. Emphasize decision hierarchy: NPV > IRR > Payback

Effective Pedagogy:
- Start simple (independent projects where metrics agree)
- Add complexity (mutually exclusive, non-standard CFs)
- Use real company examples
- Have students calculate all metrics for same project
- Discuss trade-offs and practical considerations

Critical Message:
"NPV is theoretically best, but other metrics provide useful information.
Calculate multiple metrics, but when they conflict, follow NPV."

================================================================================
SECTION 11. WEIGHTED AVERAGE COST OF CAPITAL (WACC)
================================================================================

11.1 WHAT IS WACC?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) is the average rate of return required
by ALL investors (debt and equity) in a firm or project.

Purpose:
WACC is the discount rate used to value:
- Free Cash Flows to the Firm (FCFF)
- Enterprise value
- Project cash flows (when leverage is stable)

Key Insight:
WACC represents the opportunity cost of capital for the firm's assets.

Components:
A firm has two types of investors:
1. Debt holders (lenders)
2. Equity holders (shareholders)

Each requires a return commensurate with their risk.

WACC weights these required returns by their proportions in the capital structure.

11.2 THE WACC FORMULA
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Standard Formula:
WACC = (E/V) × rE + (D/V) × rD × (1 - τ)

Where:
E = Market value of equity
D = Market value of debt
V = E + D = Total firm value (enterprise value)
rE = Cost of equity (required return on equity)
rD = Cost of debt (required return on debt)
τ = Corporate tax rate

Tax Shield:
The (1 - τ) term reflects the tax deductibility of interest.
Interest payments reduce taxable income, creating a tax shield.

Interpretation:
WACC is a weighted average where:
- Weights are proportions of firm value (E/V and D/V)
- Costs are required returns (rE and rD)
- Debt cost is after-tax because interest is tax-deductible

11.3 WHY WACC HAS TAX SHIELD
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Interest Tax Shield:
Interest expense is tax-deductible.

Example:
EBIT = $1,000
Interest = $100
Tax rate = 30%

Without Debt:
Taxable income = $1,000
Tax = $300
After-tax cash flow to investors = $700

With Debt:
Taxable income = $1,000 - $100 = $900
Tax = $270
After-tax CF = $900 - $270 = $630 (to equity)
Plus: Interest paid to debt holders = $100
Total to all investors = $630 + $100 = $730

Tax savings from debt = $30 = Interest × Tax rate

Present Value of Tax Shield:
If debt is permanent:
PV(Tax Shield) = τ × D

This is why we use after-tax cost of debt in WACC.

Effective Cost of Debt:
Before-tax cost: rD
After-tax cost: rD × (1 - τ)

Example:
If rD = 8% and τ = 30%:
After-tax cost = 8% × (1 - 0.30) = 5.6%

11.4 CALCULATING COST OF EQUITY: CAPM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Most Common Approach: Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)

CAPM Formula:
rE = rF + βE × MRP

Where:
rF = Risk-free rate
βE = Equity beta (systematic risk of equity)
MRP = Market risk premium (expected market return - risk-free rate)

Intuition:
- rF: Compensation for time value of money
- βE × MRP: Compensation for systematic risk

Components Explained:

1. Risk-Free Rate (rF):
   - Use long-term government bond yield
   - Match maturity to project/firm horizon
   - Example: 10-year Treasury yield

2. Equity Beta (βE):
   - Measures sensitivity to market movements
   - βE = 1: Moves with market
   - βE > 1: More volatile than market (higher risk)
   - βE < 1: Less volatile than market (lower risk)
   
   Estimation:
   - Regression of stock returns on market returns
   - Or use industry average
   - Or use comparable companies

3. Market Risk Premium (MRP):
   - Expected excess return of market over risk-free rate
   - Historical average: 5-8% (varies by period, geography)
   - Most practitioners use 5-7% for U.S.

Example Calculation:
rF = 3%
βE = 1.2
MRP = 6%

rE = 3% + 1.2 × 6% = 3% + 7.2% = 10.2%

11.5 CALCULATING COST OF DEBT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Cost of debt (rD) is the required return demanded by lenders.

Methods to Estimate:

METHOD 1: Yield to Maturity (YTM)
If firm has traded bonds:
rD ≈ YTM on bonds

Most direct method when available.

METHOD 2: Bank Loan Rates
If firm borrows from banks:
rD = Interest rate on loans

METHOD 3: Risk-Free Rate Plus Spread
rD = rF + Credit Spread

Credit spread depends on:
- Credit rating
- Default risk
- Industry
- Economic conditions

Example spreads:
AAA: +0.5%
AA: +1.0%
A: +1.5%
BBB: +2.5%
BB: +4%

METHOD 4: Debt Beta Approach
If debt is risky:
rD = rF + βD × MRP

Where βD = debt beta (usually << 1)

Important Distinction:
Promised yield ≥ Expected return

If default risk exists, promised yield exceeds expected return because some
promised payments may not be made.

For corporate finance, we want expected return.

Example:
Firm promises 10% on bonds.
Probability of default = 20%
If default, recover 40% of face value.

Expected return = 0.8 × 10% + 0.2 × (-60% loss) = 8% - 12% = -4%
Wait, this needs proper calculation:

Expected return = 0.8 × 10% + 0.2 × (40% - 100%) = 8% + 0.2 × (-60%) = 8% - 12% = -4%

Actually:
Expected return = 0.8 × 10% + 0.2 × [recovery - principal loss]
Better: Expected return ≈ 10% × (1 - Expected Loss Rate)

For practical purposes, if debt is investment grade (low default risk):
rD ≈ YTM

11.6 MARKET VALUES VS. BOOK VALUES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

CRITICAL: WACC requires MARKET VALUES, not book values.

Why:
Market values reflect current opportunity costs.
Book values are historical and don't reflect current prices.

Market Value of Equity:
E = Share price × Number of shares outstanding

Simple and observable for public companies.

Market Value of Debt:
More challenging:

If bonds are traded:
D = Market price of bonds × Number of bonds

If bonds aren't traded:
Approximate: Use book value as proxy
Better: Estimate present value of promised payments at current yield

Example:
Book value of debt = $100M (face value)
Current market yield for similar debt = 8%
Original coupon rate = 6%
If bonds selling below par:
Market value < Book value

Rule of Thumb:
If interest rates have risen since debt issued → Market value < Book value
If interest rates have fallen → Market value > Book value

For simplicity, many practitioners use book value of debt if:
- Debt is not publicly traded
- Debt was issued recently
- Interest rates haven't changed much

But theoretically, market value is correct.

11.7 COMPREHENSIVE WACC CALCULATION EXAMPLE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Scenario:
Calculate WACC for TechCorp.

Given Data:
- Stock price: $50 per share
- Shares outstanding: 10 million
- Equity beta: 1.3
- Bonds outstanding: $200M face value, trading at 95% of par
- Bond YTM: 7%
- Risk-free rate: 3%
- Market risk premium: 6%
- Corporate tax rate: 25%

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate Market Value of Equity
E = $50 × 10M shares = $500M

Step 2: Calculate Market Value of Debt
D = $200M × 0.95 = $190M

Step 3: Calculate Total Firm Value
V = E + D = $500M + $190M = $690M

Step 4: Calculate Weights
E/V = $500M / $690M = 72.5%
D/V = $190M / $690M = 27.5%

Step 5: Calculate Cost of Equity (using CAPM)
rE = rF + βE × MRP
rE = 3% + 1.3 × 6% = 3% + 7.8% = 10.8%

Step 6: Cost of Debt
rD = YTM = 7%

Step 7: Calculate WACC
WACC = (E/V) × rE + (D/V) × rD × (1 - τ)
WACC = 0.725 × 10.8% + 0.275 × 7% × (1 - 0.25)
WACC = 7.83% + 0.275 × 5.25%
WACC = 7.83% + 1.44%
WACC = 9.27%

Interpretation:
TechCorp's average cost of capital is 9.27%.
Projects must earn at least 9.27% to create value (NPV > 0).

Sensitivity:
What if tax rate were 30% instead?
WACC = 0.725 × 10.8% + 0.275 × 7% × (1 - 0.30)
WACC = 7.83% + 1.35% = 9.18%
(Lower tax rate slightly increases WACC because tax shield is less valuable)

11.8 WACC AND LEVERAGE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Key Principle:
As leverage (D/E) changes, WACC changes.

Two Opposing Effects:

EFFECT 1: Debt is Cheaper Than Equity
rD < rE (because debt is less risky)
→ More debt should lower WACC

EFFECT 2: Leverage Increases Equity Risk
As D/E rises, equity becomes riskier
→ rE increases, which raises WACC

Net Effect:
At low levels of leverage: Effect 1 dominates → WACC decreases
At high levels of leverage: Effect 2 dominates → WACC increases

Optimal Capital Structure:
The leverage ratio that minimizes WACC.

Graph (Conceptual):
    WACC
      |
      |     \
      |      \
      |       \___
      |           \___/  ← Minimum WACC
      |               \
      |                 \
      |____________________
                      D/E

Important Caveat:
Above analysis ignores financial distress costs.
At very high leverage, probability of bankruptcy increases, raising costs further.

11.9 LEVERAGE AND BETA
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Fundamental Relationship:
βAsset = (E/V) × βE + (D/V) × βD

This is conservation of systematic risk.
The asset beta (risk of the business) equals the weighted average of debt and
equity betas.

If debt is approximately risk-free (βD ≈ 0):
βAsset ≈ (E/V) × βE

Rearranging for equity beta:
βE = βAsset × (V/E)
βE = βAsset × (1 + D/E)  [if βD = 0]

More generally (if βD ≠ 0):
βE = βAsset + (D/E) × (βAsset - βD)

This is the RELEVERING formula.

Key Insight:
Leverage increases equity beta (and hence cost of equity) but doesn't change
asset beta (business risk is unchanged).

Example:
Unlevered firm (no debt):
βAsset = 1.0
βE = 1.0 (same as asset beta when no debt)

After leveraging to D/E = 0.5 (assuming βD = 0):
βE = 1.0 × (1 + 0.5) = 1.5
rE increases proportionally

But βAsset remains 1.0 (business risk unchanged).

11.10 UNLEVERING AND RELEVERING BETAS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Practical Application:
When estimating cost of equity for a project or firm using comparables.

Process:

STEP 1: Find Comparable Companies
Identify firms in same industry/business.

STEP 2: Unlever Their Betas
For each comparable, calculate asset beta:
βAsset = βE / (1 + (1-τ) × D/E)

Or if considering debt beta:
βAsset = [βE + βD × (D/E)] / [1 + D/E]

STEP 3: Average Asset Betas
βAsset, average = Average of unlevered betas from comparables

STEP 4: Relever Using Target Capital Structure
Using target D/E for the project/firm:
βE, target = βAsset × [1 + (1-τ) × (D/E)target]

STEP 5: Calculate Cost of Equity
rE = rF + βE, target × MRP

STEP 6: Calculate WACC
Using target capital structure.

Why This Works:
- Asset beta reflects business risk (independent of capital structure)
- Equity beta reflects both business risk and financial risk (leverage)
- By unlevering and relevering, we separate these effects

Example:

Comparable firms in retail industry:
Company A: βE = 1.2, D/E = 0.3, τ = 25%
Company B: βE = 1.4, D/E = 0.5, τ = 25%
Company C: βE = 1.1, D/E = 0.2, τ = 25%

Unlever each:
βA,asset = 1.2 / [1 + (1-0.25) × 0.3] = 1.2 / 1.225 = 0.98
βB,asset = 1.4 / [1 + 0.75 × 0.5] = 1.4 / 1.375 = 1.02
βC,asset = 1.1 / [1 + 0.75 × 0.2] = 1.1 / 1.15 = 0.96

Average: βAsset = (0.98 + 1.02 + 0.96) / 3 = 0.99

Now, relevering for our firm with D/E = 0.4:
βE,target = 0.99 × [1 + 0.75 × 0.4] = 0.99 × 1.30 = 1.29

Use this for cost of equity:
If rF = 3% and MRP = 6%:
rE = 3% + 1.29 × 6% = 10.74%

11.11 PROJECT-SPECIFIC WACC
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Key Principle:
Use discount rate appropriate to the risk of the cash flows.

Implication:
Do NOT automatically use firm-wide WACC for all projects.

When Firm WACC is Appropriate:
- Project has same risk as firm's average operations
- Project has similar leverage as firm
- Project is in firm's core business

When Project-Specific WACC is Needed:
- Project in different industry (different business risk)
- Project has different operating leverage
- Project requires different capital structure

Process for Project WACC:

STEP 1: Identify project's business risk
Find comparable firms in project's industry.

STEP 2: Estimate project's asset beta
Unlever comparables' betas, average them.

STEP 3: Determine project's target leverage
May differ from firm's leverage.

STEP 4: Calculate project's equity beta
Relever asset beta using project's target leverage.

STEP 5: Calculate project WACC
Using project-specific rE and capital structure.

Example:

TechCorp (software, WACC = 10%) considering opening retail stores.

Firm WACC: 10%
Project (retail) WACC: ?

Retail comparables have average βAsset = 0.8
TechCorp's βAsset = 1.3

Using firm WACC (10%) for retail project would be WRONG.
Retail is lower risk than software.

Correct approach:
βRetail,asset = 0.8 (from comparables)
Relever using TechCorp's leverage: βE = 0.8 × (1 + D/E)
Calculate rE,retail = rF + βE × MRP
Calculate WACCretail < 10%

Result: Retail project should use lower discount rate than firm average.

11.12 MARGINAL vs. AVERAGE WACC
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Marginal WACC:
Cost of raising one additional dollar of capital.

Average WACC:
Weighted average cost of all capital currently in place.

For Valuation: Use marginal WACC
(the cost of new capital for new projects)

Why:
New projects are funded with new capital, not old capital.

Practical Issue:
Marginal and average WACC are often similar, so many use average as proxy.

When They Differ:
- Interest rates have changed since debt was issued
- Firm's credit rating has changed
- Capital structure has shifted

Example:
Firm issued debt 5 years ago at 6%.
Current market rate for similar debt: 8%.

Average rD (including old debt): 6.5%
Marginal rD (for new debt): 8%

For new projects: Use 8%, not 6.5%.

11.13 WACC LIMITATIONS AND ALTERNATIVES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

WACC Assumes:
1. Constant leverage (D/V ratio remains stable)
2. Perpetual debt (tax shields are permanent)
3. No financial distress costs
4. Same risk for all cash flows

When WACC Fails:

SITUATION 1: Changing Leverage Over Time
If D/V ratio changes significantly:
→ Use Adjusted Present Value (APV) method instead

APV = NPV(unlevered) + PV(Tax Shields) - PV(Financial Distress Costs)

SITUATION 2: Project Has Different Risk Than Firm
→ Use project-specific WACC (covered above)

SITUATION 3: Tax Rate Changes
If τ changes over time:
→ Adjust WACC for each period or use APV

SITUATION 4: Firm is Not Public (No Equity Beta)
→ Use industry average beta or comparables

Alternatives to WACC:

1. APV (Adjusted Present Value):
   - Value project as if all-equity
   - Add value of tax shields separately
   - Useful when leverage changes

2. FCFE (Flow to Equity):
   - Discount cash flows to equity at rE
   - Use when directly valuing equity (not entire firm)

3. Risk-Adjusted Discount Rates:
   - Different discount rates for different components
   - Use when cash flows have very different risk profiles

Bottom Line:
WACC is the workhorse but not universal. Use appropriate method for the situation.

11.14 COMMON WACC CALCULATION ERRORS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

ERROR 1: Using Book Values Instead of Market Values
WRONG: D = book value of debt
RIGHT: D = market value of debt (or proxy with book if unavailable)

ERROR 2: Forgetting Tax Shield on Debt
WRONG: WACC = (E/V) × rE + (D/V) × rD
RIGHT: WACC = (E/V) × rE + (D/V) × rD × (1 - τ)

ERROR 3: Using Coupon Rate as Cost of Debt
WRONG: rD = coupon rate on bonds
RIGHT: rD = current YTM or market rate for similar debt

ERROR 4: Using Firm Beta Without Adjusting for Leverage
WRONG: Use βE from comparable with different leverage
RIGHT: Unlever, then relever using target leverage

ERROR 5: Weights Don't Sum to 1
Check: (E/V) + (D/V) should equal 1.0

ERROR 6: Inconsistent Tax Rates
If debt is after-tax and equity is pre-tax, comparison is invalid.
Use same tax treatment.

ERROR 7: Using Historical MRP Instead of Forward-Looking
WRONG: MRP based on past 100 years
BETTER: MRP based on current expectations or recent history

ERROR 8: Circular Reasoning in Valuation
V = PV(CFs at WACC)
But WACC depends on V (through weights E/V and D/V)!

Solution: Iterate until consistent.

11.15 COMPREHENSIVE PROBLEM: WACC FOR PROJECT EVALUATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Scenario:
Manufacturing company evaluating new product line.

Firm Data:
- Current stock price: $40
- Shares outstanding: 50M
- Market value of debt: $800M
- Firm's equity beta: 1.5
- Firm's D/E ratio: 0.8
- YTM on firm's bonds: 6%
- Tax rate: 30%

Market Data:
- Risk-free rate: 4%
- Market risk premium: 5.5%

Project Data:
- Expected to have same risk as firm's existing operations
- Will maintain firm's target leverage (D/E = 0.8)

Question: What WACC should be used for this project?

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate Market Value of Equity
E = $40 × 50M = $2,000M

Step 2: Market Value of Debt (given)
D = $800M

Step 3: Total Firm Value
V = E + D = $2,000M + $800M = $2,800M

Step 4: Verify D/E ratio
D/E = $800M / $2,000M = 0.4

Wait, problem states D/E = 0.8. Let me recalculate.

If D/E = 0.8, then D = 0.8E
Given D = $800M:
E = $800M / 0.8 = $1,000M

This means stock price × shares ≠ given values. Use the D/E ratio as more
reliable for this problem.

Recalculating:
D = $800M
D/E = 0.8, so E = $800M / 0.8 = $1,000M
V = $1,800M

Weights:
D/V = $800M / $1,800M = 44.4%
E/V = $1,000M / $1,800M = 55.6%

Step 5: Calculate Cost of Equity
βE = 1.5 (given)
rE = rF + βE × MRP
rE = 4% + 1.5 × 5.5% = 4% + 8.25% = 12.25%

Step 6: Cost of Debt
rD = 6% (YTM given)

Step 7: Calculate WACC
WACC = (E/V) × rE + (D/V) × rD × (1 - τ)
WACC = 0.556 × 12.25% + 0.444 × 6% × (1 - 0.30)
WACC = 6.81% + 0.444 × 4.2%
WACC = 6.81% + 1.86%
WACC = 8.67%

Answer: Use 8.67% as the discount rate for this project.

Check:
- Project has same risk as firm → Use firm's betas
- Project maintains firm's leverage → Use firm's weights
- Therefore, use firm's WACC

If project had different risk, we'd need to unlever firm's beta, use
comparable companies, and re-calculate.

11.16 COMMON STUDENT MISCONCEPTIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

MISCONCEPTION 1: "More debt always lowers WACC"
REALITY: While debt is cheaper, it increases equity risk (βE and rE rise).
Net effect depends on leverage level.

MISCONCEPTION 2: "WACC is the return the firm earns"
REALITY: WACC is the return REQUIRED by investors. Actual return may differ.

MISCONCEPTION 3: "Use historical returns to estimate WACC components"
REALITY: WACC requires forward-looking expected returns, not past returns.

MISCONCEPTION 4: "All projects in a firm should use same WACC"
REALITY: Projects with different risk require different discount rates.

MISCONCEPTION 5: "Book value and market value of debt are same"
REALITY: They can differ significantly if interest rates have changed.

MISCONCEPTION 6: "Higher WACC is better for the firm"
REALITY: Higher WACC means capital is more expensive. Lower WACC (without
increasing risk) is better.

MISCONCEPTION 7: "Tax shield makes debt always better than equity"
REALITY: Excessive debt increases financial distress costs and equity risk.

11.17 TEACHING NOTES FOR WACC
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

When teaching WACC:
1. Start with intuition: Average cost of all capital sources
2. Explain why weights must be market values
3. Derive formula component by component
4. Explain tax shield carefully (most confusing part)
5. Work through complete numerical examples
6. Show sensitivity to leverage
7. Explain unlevering/relevering (crucial practical skill)
8. Discuss project-specific vs. firm-wide WACC
9. Connect back to NPV (WACC is the discount rate)

Effective Pedagogy:
- Use visualization: Pie chart showing E/V and D/V
- Show WACC calculation in spreadsheet step-by-step
- Compare WACC for firms with different leverage
- Practice unlevering/relevering with real companies
- Discuss when WACC is appropriate vs. alternatives

Common Teaching Challenges:
1. Students confuse book vs. market values
2. Tax shield concept is unintuitive
3. Beta levering/unlevering formulas are complex
4. Circular reasoning in valuation (V depends on WACC, WACC depends on V)

Solutions:
1. Emphasize market values repeatedly with examples
2. Show tax shield benefit numerically
3. Work through levering/unlevering multiple times
4. Explain iteration or using target weights

Critical Concepts to Emphasize:
- WACC reflects opportunity cost of capital
- Higher risk → Higher WACC → Lower PV
- Cannot change WACC to make project look better
- WACC assumes constant leverage
- Use project-specific WACC when appropriate
