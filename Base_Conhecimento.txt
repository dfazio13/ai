SECTION 0. COURSE CONTEXT AND LEARNING OBJECTIVES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This knowledge base supports an applied corporate finance course focused on
investment decision-making, valuation, and capital budgeting.

Core learning objectives:
1. Understand how firms create value.
2. Learn how to evaluate investment projects using cash flows.
3. Master discounting, NPV, and alternative decision rules.
4. Understand why NPV is the dominant criterion in theory and practice.
5. Learn how to construct project cash flows from financial statements.
6. Understand the role of risk, financing, inflation, and opportunity cost.

================================================================================
SECTION 1. WHAT IS CORPORATE FINANCE?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Corporate finance studies how firms allocate resources over time under uncertainty.
Every decision made by a firm has financial implications.

Definition (Damodaran):
Every decision that affects the finances of a business is a corporate finance decision.

Corporate finance is typically organized around three questions:
1. Investment decisions: Which projects should the firm undertake?
2. Financing decisions: How should the firm fund these projects?
3. Payout and restructuring decisions: How should cash be returned to investors?

Examples of corporate finance decisions:
- Launching a new product
- Buying or selling a factory
- Mergers and acquisitions
- Bankruptcy and restructuring
- Dividend and share repurchase policy

================================================================================
SECTION 2. THE OBJECTIVE OF THE FIRM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Standard assumption:
The objective of the firm is to maximize firm value.

This assumption:
- Is descriptive of how capital markets reward managers
- Provides a clear and consistent decision rule
- Aligns managerial incentives with shareholder wealth

Value creation principle:
A firm creates value when the present value of benefits exceeds the present value
of costs.

Bad decisions:
- Can increase revenues but destroy value
- Can be profitable in accounting terms but negative in economic terms

================================================================================
SECTION 3. CASH FLOWS AND VALUE CREATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Value depends on cash flows, not accounting profits.

Why cash flows?
- Investors consume cash, not accounting earnings
- Accounting numbers include non-cash items
- Accounting rules vary across firms and countries

Relevant cash flows:
- Incremental
- After-tax
- Expected (not realized)
- Reflect opportunity costs

================================================================================
SECTION 4. TIME VALUE OF MONEY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow.

Reasons:
1. Opportunity cost: money can be invested
2. Risk: future cash flows are uncertain
3. Inflation: purchasing power declines over time

Discounting converts future cash flows into equivalent present values.

================================================================================
SECTION 5. PRESENT VALUE (PV)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Definition:
The present value is the value today of a stream of future cash flows.

Formula:
PV = Σ Ct / (1 + r)^t

Where:
- Ct = cash flow at time t
- r = discount rate

Interpretation:
PV answers the question:
"What is this future stream of cash flows worth today?"

================================================================================
SECTION 6. DISCOUNT RATE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The discount rate reflects:
- Time value of money
- Risk of the cash flows

Interpretation:
The discount rate is the opportunity cost of capital.

Higher risk → higher discount rate → lower present value.

================================================================================
SECTION 7. PERPETUITIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
A perpetuity is a constant cash flow that continues forever.

PV of perpetuity:
PV = C / r

Economic intuition:
- Useful for long-lived assets
- Useful terminal value approximation

Important:
Perpetuity does NOT mean infinite life in practice.
For horizons above ~50 periods, perpetuity is an excellent approximation.

================================================================================
SECTION 8. GROWING PERPETUITIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Cash flows that grow at a constant rate g forever.

PV = C / (r - g)

Conditions:
- r > g
- Growth rate must be sustainable

Used extensively in:
- Firm valuation
- Terminal value calculations

================================================================================
SECTION 9. ANNUITIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
An annuity is a constant cash flow for a finite number of periods.

PV of annuity:
PV = (C / r) * [1 - 1 / (1 + r)^T]

Applications:
- Mortgages
- Leases
- Bonds

================================================================================
SECTION 10. NET PRESENT VALUE (NPV)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Definition:
NPV is the present value of all cash flows, including the initial investment.

Formula:
NPV = -I + Σ Ct / (1 + r)^t

Decision rule:
- Accept if NPV > 0
- Reject if NPV < 0

Economic meaning:
NPV measures the increase in total firm value from undertaking a project.

================================================================================
SECTION 11. WHY NPV WORKS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
NPV:
- Accounts for time value of money
- Accounts for risk
- Uses all cash flows
- Is consistent with value maximization

NPV is additive:
- The NPV of multiple projects equals the sum of individual NPVs

================================================================================
SECTION 12. INTERNAL RATE OF RETURN (IRR)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Definition:
IRR is the discount rate that sets NPV equal to zero.

Decision rule:
Accept if IRR > cost of capital.

IRR is intuitive because it is expressed in percentage terms.

================================================================================
SECTION 13. PROBLEMS WITH IRR
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Multiple IRRs:
Occurs when cash flow signs change more than once.

2. No IRR:
Occurs when NPV never crosses zero.

3. Mutually exclusive projects:
IRR may rank projects incorrectly.

4. Scale problem:
High IRR small projects vs low IRR large projects.

5. Timing problem:
Different cash flow timing can distort rankings.

Conclusion:
IRR should never override NPV.

================================================================================
SECTION 14. PAYBACK PERIOD
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Definition:
Time required to recover initial investment from cash flows.

Advantages:
- Simple
- Measures liquidity risk

Disadvantages:
- Ignores time value of money
- Ignores risk
- Ignores cash flows after payback

Discounted payback partially fixes the first problem.

================================================================================
SECTION 15. ACCOUNTING-BASED MEASURES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Examples:
- Return on Assets
- Accounting Rate of Return

Problems:
- Based on accounting profits
- Ignore time value of money
- Ignore risk

================================================================================
SECTION 16. CAPITAL BUDGETING PRINCIPLES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Incremental cash flows:
Cash flows with the project minus cash flows without the project.

Ignore:
- Sunk costs (past costs)

Include:
- Opportunity costs
- Cannibalization effects
- Side effects

================================================================================
SECTION 17. FREE CASH FLOW TO THE FIRM (FCFF)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
FCFF represents cash generated by assets, independent of financing.

Formula:
FCFF = EBIT(1 - t) + Depreciation - CAPEX - ΔNWC

Used for:
- Project valuation
- Firm valuation

================================================================================
SECTION 18. EBIT VS EBITDA METHODS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
EBIT method:
FCF = EBIT(1 - t) + Depreciation - CAPEX - ΔNWC

EBITDA method:
FCF = EBITDA(1 - t) + t*Depreciation - CAPEX - ΔNWC

Both produce identical results when done correctly.

================================================================================
SECTION 19. DEPRECIATION AND TAX SHIELDS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Depreciation:
- Non-cash
- Reduces taxable income
- Creates tax shields

Tax shield:
Tax rate × depreciation

================================================================================
SECTION 20. WORKING CAPITAL
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Net Working Capital (NWC):
Inventory + Cash + Receivables - Payables

Increases in NWC:
- Reduce cash flow

Recovery of NWC at project end:
- Positive cash flow

================================================================================
SECTION 21. SALVAGE VALUE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Cash flow from asset disposal.

Tax treatment depends on comparison between:
- Sale price
- Book value

================================================================================
SECTION 22. SUNK COSTS AND OPPORTUNITY COSTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Sunk costs:
- Already incurred
- Irrelevant

Opportunity costs:
- Value of best alternative use
- Must be included

================================================================================
SECTION 23. FINANCING COSTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Interest, dividends, and principal repayments:
- Are financing cash flows
- Should NOT be included in project cash flows

They are captured in the discount rate (WACC).

================================================================================
SECTION 24. INFLATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Nominal cash flows:
- Include inflation

Real cash flows:
- Exclude inflation

Consistency rule:
Nominal cash flows → nominal discount rate
Real cash flows → real discount rate

Approximation:
Nominal rate ≈ real rate + inflation

================================================================================
SECTION 25. SUMMARY
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- Value depends on discounted cash flows
- NPV is the primary decision rule
- Cash flows must be incremental and after-tax
- Financing affects discount rates, not cash flows
- Inflation consistency is critical


APPLIED CORPORATE FINANCE – COMPREHENSIVE KNOWLEDGE BASE
SESSION 2: WEIGHTED AVERAGE COST OF CAPITAL (WACC)
Professor Dimas Fazio – National University of Singapore Business School

================================================================================
SECTION 0. PURPOSE OF THIS MODULE
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
This module explains how to compute the correct discount rate for risky projects and firms.
It builds directly on capital budgeting and NPV.

Core questions answered:
1. What is WACC and why does it matter?
2. How does risk affect discount rates?
3. How do we compute WACC in practice?
4. How does leverage affect risk and beta?
5. When should we NOT use WACC?

================================================================================
SECTION 1. WHAT IS WACC?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) is the discount rate used to value
a firm’s or project’s free cash flows to the firm (FCFF).

These cash flows are paid to:
- Equity holders
- Debt holders

WACC reflects:
1. The systematic (non-diversifiable) risk of cash flows
2. The tax advantage of debt financing (interest tax shields)

Higher systematic risk → higher WACC
More debt (holding risk constant) → lower WACC due to tax shields

================================================================================
SECTION 2. KEY ASSUMPTION BEHIND WACC
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WACC assumes the firm maintains a constant target capital structure.

Target leverage:
- D / V = target debt-to-enterprise-value ratio
- V = D + E (enterprise value)
- D and E should be market values (not book values)

If leverage is NOT expected to remain stable:
→ WACC should NOT be used

================================================================================
SECTION 3. THE WACC FORMULA
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WACC = (D / V) × rD × (1 − τ) + (E / V) × rE

Where:
- rD = cost of debt
- rE = cost of equity
- τ = corporate tax rate

Interpretation:
- Debt is cheaper than equity due to tax deductibility of interest
- Equity is riskier and requires a higher expected return

================================================================================
SECTION 4. WACC AND RISK
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
WACC increases with:
- Higher business risk
- Higher exposure to economic cycles
- Higher equity beta

WACC decreases with:
- Higher tax rates
- Moderate increases in leverage (before distress costs)

Important:
Managers cannot reduce systematic risk by changing leverage.
Leverage redistributes risk between debt and equity.

================================================================================
SECTION 5. THREE WAYS TO COMPUTE WACC
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. Given cost of equity and cost of debt
2. Given equity and debt betas (via CAPM)
3. Using comparable companies

All three approaches should give consistent answers if done correctly.

================================================================================
SECTION 6. COST OF EQUITY (CAPM)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Cost of equity is typically estimated using CAPM:

rE = rF + βE × MRP

Where:
- rF = risk-free rate
- βE = equity beta
- MRP = market risk premium

Equity beta measures exposure to systematic market risk.

================================================================================
SECTION 7. COST OF DEBT
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The cost of debt is the expected return required by lenders.

In practice:
- Often approximated using the yield to maturity (YTM) of outstanding bonds
- Or the borrowing rate on bank loans
- Or risk-free rate plus a default spread

Important:
Promised interest rate ≥ expected return to lenders (if default risk exists)

================================================================================
SECTION 8. MARKET RISK PREMIUM (MRP)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
The market risk premium is the expected excess return of the market over the risk-free rate.

Common estimation approaches:
1. Historical averages (most common)
2. Surveys of investors
3. Dividend growth models (least common)

MRP is the least precisely estimated input in WACC.

================================================================================
SECTION 9. BETA: ECONOMIC MEANING
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Beta measures systematic risk:
- Sensitivity of asset returns to market returns

Beta does NOT measure:
- Total volatility
- Firm-specific risk (which can be diversified)

Beta is estimated using regressions, but estimates are noisy.

================================================================================
SECTION 10. LEVERAGE AND SYSTEMATIC RISK
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Key principle: Conservation of systematic risk

Asset (unlevered) beta:
βA = (E/V)βE + (D/V)βD

Leverage increases equity beta:
βE = βU + (D/E)(βU − βD)

If debt is approximately risk-free:
βE ≈ βU × (1 + D/E)

Interpretation:
Leverage magnifies equity risk without changing asset risk.

================================================================================
SECTION 11. UNLEVERING AND RELEVERING BETAS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Step 1: Unlever comparable firm beta
βU = (E/V)βE + (D/V)βD

Step 2: Relever using target capital structure
βE,target = βU + (D/E)target × (βU − βD)

This allows estimation of project or firm-specific discount rates.

================================================================================
SECTION 12. USING COMPARABLE COMPANIES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
When a firm lacks reliable beta estimates:
- Use comparable firms in the same industry
- Unlever their betas
- Average asset betas
- Relever using target capital structure

This is standard practice in valuation.

================================================================================
SECTION 13. PROJECT DISCOUNT RATES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Rule:
Cash flows must be discounted at a rate appropriate to their risk.

Implication:
- Do NOT automatically use firm WACC for all projects
- Projects with different risk profiles require different discount rates

Otherwise:
Identical projects would have different values depending on ownership,
which is inconsistent with market pricing.

================================================================================
SECTION 14. TAX ADJUSTMENTS AND LIMITATIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Standard WACC:
- Accounts only for corporate taxes
- Ignores investor-level taxes

WACC assumes:
- Constant leverage
- Stable tax shields

If leverage changes over time:
→ Use APV or FCFE instead

================================================================================
SECTION 15. PRACTICAL INPUT CHOICES
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Risk-free rate:
- Long-term government bonds for long-term projects

Debt:
- Market value if available
- Book value as proxy if not traded

Equity:
- Market capitalization

Beta:
- Prefer industry averages over individual estimates

================================================================================
SECTION 16. ACCURACY AND LIMITATIONS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Sources of error:
- Beta estimation error (±0.3 common)
- MRP uncertainty
- CAPM model limitations

Despite imperfections:
WACC remains the workhorse discount rate in corporate finance.

================================================================================
SECTION 17. KEY TAKEAWAYS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
- WACC is the correct discount rate for FCFF when leverage is stable
- Risk determines discount rates
- Leverage reallocates risk but does not eliminate it
- Comparable firms are critical in practice
- Using the wrong discount rate destroys value
